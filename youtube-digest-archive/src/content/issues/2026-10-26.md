# America's Electoral Fortress: How the Vote Withstood a Campaign of Distrust

Election Day, for many Americans, has morphed into an election season, a period fraught with anticipation and, increasingly, anxiety. For years, the integrity of the nation's voting system has been under relentless scrutiny, particularly due to a concerted campaign by former President Donald Trump and his allies to sow doubt and undermine public confidence. From challenging states' authority over voter rolls to attempting to change rules on mail-in ballots, the rhetoric has been loud, persistent, and often confusing.

But beneath the surface noise, a quieter, more determined battle has been waged—and largely won—by voting rights advocates and local election officials. The question remains: how secure is our voting system, and what is the true impact of this sustained assault on democratic trust?

To separate fact from fiction, *America, Actually* spoke with Eliza Sweren-Becker, the deputy director for the voting rights program at the Brennan Center for Justice. Her assessment offers a surprising degree of reassurance, tempered by a clear-eyed view of ongoing challenges.

## Debunking the "Stolen" Narrative

The pervasive fear that elections could be "stolen" or unfairly manipulated has been a cornerstone of Trump's narrative. Yet, Sweren-Becker contends that the immediate risk to free and fair elections is "relatively low." This confidence stems from a series of decisive legal and administrative victories against efforts to meddle with voting processes.

### A String of Legal Victories

The Trump administration's attempts to interfere with election administration have been met with a formidable legal defense, often resulting in resounding defeats. Sweren-Becker highlights a significant "winning streak" for voting rights advocates.

"The president over the last two years issued two executive orders that attempted to meddle with the way that states run elections," she explains. One aimed to alter the federal voter registration form, potentially requiring documents like passports, while the other sought to interfere with mail-in ballot procedures. Both were swiftly halted by courts as "unlawful," underscoring a fundamental principle: "The president does not have a role in administering elections or setting the rules for elections."

Further illustrating this point, the Department of Justice, acting on presidential directives, sued 30 states and Washington, D.C., for their unredacted voter files. The outcome was a stark rebuke: "DOJ is on a 26-case losing streak. All 26 courts have decided that the Department of Justice is not entitled to those records." These repeated legal setbacks demonstrate that the administration's attempts to overstep its authority in election matters have been consistently rebuffed.

### The Strength of Local Control

A crucial, yet often overlooked, safeguard of American elections is their highly decentralized nature. Unlike many other nations, the U.S. does not have a single, centralized federal election system. Instead, elections are largely a local and state enterprise, managed by thousands of dedicated officials across the country.

"One of the strengths of our election system across this country is actually its decentralization," Sweren-Becker notes. This dispersed structure makes widespread, coordinated interference incredibly difficult. Local and state election officials work year-round to prepare for and run elections, possessing an intimate understanding of their communities' needs and processes. When the executive branch has attempted to interfere, courts have consistently affirmed that election rules are the purview of the states, not the federal government.

### Dispelling Myths: Non-Citizen Voting and Software Glitches

Amidst the claims of widespread fraud, specific allegations often surface, such as large numbers of non-citizens voting or software errors compromising voter rolls. These claims, Sweren-Becker clarifies, are largely inaccurate or mischaracterized.

For instance, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) once claimed to have found over 250,000 non-citizens registered in a handful of states. However, Sweren-Becker reveals that the administration's "own data" does not support these claims. States like Louisiana and Utah, after cross-referencing their voter rolls with federal citizenship data, found no significant problem. "We know that in this country only US citizens vote with vanishingly rare exceptions," she states, adding that the few instances of non-citizen voting are typically "statistical nullities" and usually due to "a mistake." The idea that a non-citizen would intentionally jeopardize their residency and liberty for a single illegal ballot is, she posits, inherently unlikely.

Even when errors occur, they often demonstrate the system's built-in safeguards. New Jersey Governor Phil Murphy, for example, announced that over 6,500 non-citizens were added to the state's voter rolls due to a DMV software error, with about 400 having voted. While Trump seized on this as proof of a widespread problem, Sweren-Becker views it differently: "The fact that folks were identified because of an error at the DMV shows the system working." Election officials constantly maintain voter rolls, identifying and legally removing ineligible individuals. Such incidents, while requiring attention, highlight the system's capacity for self-correction rather than systemic vulnerability.

## The Battle for Voting Access

While direct interference attempts have largely failed, the broader campaign has also pushed for legislative changes that would fundamentally alter voting access.

### The Failed "SAVE Act" and its Broad Impact

One of Trump's top legislative priorities was the "SAVE Act," a bill the Brennan Center called "the most restrictive voting bill ever." Sweren-Becker outlines its core requirement: "a bill that would require all American citizens to produce something like a passport or a birth certificate when they register to vote, every time they register and when they re-register."

While seemingly aimed at non-citizen voting, the impact would have been far more sweeping. Research shows that "more than 21 million Americans do not have ready access to those documents." This includes students, rural voters, and individuals whose current names don't match old documents. Obtaining these documents can be costly and time-consuming, creating a significant barrier to the ballot box for millions of eligible citizens. The SAVE Act's failure in Congress, Sweren-Becker notes, was a testament to widespread public opposition, even among some Republicans, who understood its broad, restrictive implications.

### The Unmet Promise of Federal Protections

The legislative landscape for voting rights has also seen significant struggles on the proactive side. Democrats' failure to pass landmark legislation like the Freedom to Vote Act and the John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act has left critical gaps, leading to a "wave after wave of restrictive voting legislation" in states.

These federal bills would have established national standards for voter access, addressing issues like voter ID policies (e.g., New Hampshire not accepting student IDs) and making it easier for eligible Americans to participate. Their absence has allowed a "patchwork" of state laws to emerge, often making voting more difficult depending on where one lives.

### Gerrymandering and the Weakening Voting Rights Act

Beyond access, the very structure of representation is under threat. The Supreme Court, Sweren-Becker explains, has been "chipping away at the Voting Rights Act to the point where it is um sort of barely a husk of itself." This erosion began in 2013 with the gutting of the VRA's pre-clearance regime, which required states with a history of racial discrimination to clear voting changes. Later decisions, like *Brnovich* (2021) and *Colay* (2022), have further weakened the act, making it harder for voters to seek remedies in court and effectively blessing partisan gerrymandering.

The *Colay* decision is particularly concerning, as it essentially "bless[es] partisan gerrymandering... essentially saying that partisan gerrymandering is a reasonable basis or principle by which state legislatures should draw their lines." This has led to a perverse situation where "elected representatives are choosing their voters rather than voters are choosing their elected representatives," undermining the fundamental premise of representative democracy.

## Navigating Election Day and Beyond

With the midterms approaching, understanding the realities of election timing and individual responsibilities is key to maintaining confidence.

### Patience is a Virtue: Understanding Ballot Counting Timelines

One common source of anxiety, and a target for misinformation, is the time it takes to count ballots. "You're absolutely right that we may get results out of states or in certain races in the days following election day," Sweren-Becker cautions, reminding voters not to expect final results on election night.

This delay is not a sign of malfeasance but a function of process. Many states allow ballots cast by Election Day to arrive later, and ensuring every properly cast ballot is counted takes time, especially given the "limited resources" of local election officials. While states could implement policies to speed up processing, the priority, Sweren-Becker stresses, is accuracy and inclusivity: "We need to count every vote and every person's vote needs to be counted."

### Your Role in a Confident Election

For individuals concerned about election security, Sweren-Becker offers practical advice:
*   **Know Your Options:** If voting by mail, understand alternatives like ballot drop boxes or dropping off at election offices, especially if concerned about mail delays.
*   **Check Your Registration:** Ensure your voter registration is up-to-date with your current address and that you are still on the rolls. Register if you still have time.
*   **Participate Joyfully:** Voting, she emphasizes, should be a "joyful" civic experience. "Our democracy works best the more of us that can participate." Bringing friends or family, getting a sticker – these small acts reinforce community engagement and trust.

## The Lingering Shadow of Distrust

While many of the most egregious attempts at direct interference have been mitigated, the broader impact of the sustained campaign against election integrity cannot be ignored.

### The True Cost of Undermining Confidence

"The anxiety is the point," Sweren-Becker observes. The consistent questioning of election results, the unsupported claims of fraud, and the constant rhetoric of a "rigged system" are designed to create a "lack of confidence" and to "discourage voters from participating." Even if the system itself remains secure, the perception of insecurity can be damaging.

The only way to defeat this manufactured distrust, she argues, is through active participation. "The only way that that campaign to undermine our confidence and to sow distrust actually works if is if voters stay home." By turning out, regardless of party, voters affirm their belief in the democratic process and reject attempts to instill fear.

### Rebuilding Trust: Paths to a Stronger Democracy

To truly inspire trust, Sweren-Becker suggests several key reforms:

*   **National Floor for Voter Access:** Congress could establish basic federal standards, such as same-day registration, online voter registration, and universal access to mail voting (without strict excuse requirements). This would ensure that "someone's right to vote doesn't depend on what state they live on."
*   **Ban Partisan Gerrymandering:** A federal ban on partisan gerrymandering would ensure fairer representation and prevent elected officials from choosing their voters.
*   **Restore the Voting Rights Act:** Congress has the power to restore and strengthen the VRA, enacting a "statutory right to vote that is meaningfully enforceable" so that voters can seek effective legal remedies when their rights are burdened.

The anxiety that permeated discussions around election security throughout the year has, paradoxically, fueled the vigilance that has mitigated many of the worst potential impacts. The American voting system, with its decentralized nature and the tireless work of election officials and voting rights advocates, has proven remarkably resilient. While the former president's campaign of distrust has chipped away at public confidence, it has not, in large part, managed to fundamentally compromise the mechanics of the vote.

The enduring lesson is that democracy is not a given; it is a process, constantly defended and nurtured. The fight against manufactured distrust continues, but the most powerful counter-measure remains what it has always been: active, confident participation by every eligible citizen.
