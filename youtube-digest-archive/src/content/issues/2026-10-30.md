# The Sanctuary in the Storm: How World War II Shaped the Whimsical, Dark Universe of the Moomins

To the uninitiated, Moomintroll looks like the gentle mascot of a utopian fever dream. Round, bright white, with a bulbous snout reminiscent of a hippopotamus, the rounded belly of a teddy bear, and wide, expressive eyes, he ambles through a idyllic Nordic valley filled with cozy towers, blooming wildflowers, and soft summer breezes. Beside him sit his devoted Moominmamma—never without her black handbag and striped apron—his adventurous Moominpappa, and an eccentric cast of forest companions. To generations of children around the world, the Moomins are a comforting fixture of childhood, a Scandinavian cousin to Winnie the Pooh.

Yet step closer to the canvas, and the sunlight in Moominvalley casts surprisingly long, haunting shadows. 

Beneath the cozy teatimes and seaside wanderings lies an unmistakable existential gravity: creeping floods that swallow homes, terrifying fiery comets hurtling through the sky, cold specters that freeze the ground beneath their feet, and pervasive themes of exile, loneliness, and grief. 

This tension between comfort and catastrophe is no accident. The world of the Moomins was not born in a tranquil garden, but in the damp air-raid shelters of Helsinki as Soviet bombs rained down on Finland during World War II. Its creator, Tove Jansson, forged this mythical sanctuary not as an escape from reality, but as a psychological lifeline to survive it.

---

## An Artistic Dynasty in a Fractured Land

To understand Tove Jansson, one must understand the bohemian, high-wire world in which she was raised. Born in Helsinki in 1914, Tove was the eldest child of two prominent artists. Her father, Viktor “Fafan” Jansson, was a acclaimed sculptor whose fiery temper and political passions ran deep. Her mother, Signe “Ham” Hammarsten-Jansson, was a Swedish-born illustrator, graphic designer, and the family’s primary breadwinner, quietly drawing thousands of postage stamps and book covers while juggling domestic life.

In the Jansson household, art was not a hobby; it was the atmosphere itself. Plaster dust hovered in the living room air, the smell of turpentine was omnipresent, and Tove was treated as a peer and fellow artist almost from infancy. She shared a profound, almost symbiotic bond with her mother, often sitting at the kitchen table beside Ham, drawing side by side into the late hours of the night. By age 14, Tove had already illustrated her first national magazine cover.

```
       [ Viktor "Fafan" Jansson ] <---> [ Signe "Ham" Hammarsten ]
            (Sculptor, Nationalist)           (Illustrator, Swedish)
                       |                            |
                       +--------------+-------------+
                                      |
                              [ Tove Jansson ]
                         (Born 1914, Helsinki)
```

However, this artistic sanctuary was perpetually shadowed by war. When Tove was just three years old, Finland declared independence from the Russian Empire, plunging immediately into a brutal civil war between the communist "Reds" and the nationalist "Whites." Viktor Jansson enlisted enthusiastically for the Whites, prepared, as he wrote to his wife, to "joyfully die for the cause." Though he survived, the war shattered his nerves. He returned home a hardened, melancholic man who detested communism, drank heavily, and harbored severe, lifelong trauma.

When World War II erupted two decades later, Finland found itself trapped in a nightmarish geopolitical vice between two totalitarian giants: Stalin’s Soviet Union and Hitler’s Nazi Germany. For Viktor Jansson, siding with Germany was a necessary evil to defend Finnish independence against the Red Army. But for young Tove, political fanaticism of any kind was anathema.

## Satire in the Shadow of the Air Raids

As the Continuation War ignited in June 1941, Tove found herself deeply estranged from her father’s pro-German sympathies. While her younger brother was sent to the frontline, Tove used her pen as a weapon of defiance. She became a principal cartoonist for *Garm*, a Finnish political satire magazine, producing extraordinarily brave artwork that routinely pushed the boundaries of state censorship.

In her drawings, she depicted Adolf Hitler as an oversized, spoiled toddler throwing a mid-tantrum crying fit for more cake, and unmasked Joseph Stalin as a duplicitous villain. Yet, despite her fierce artistic courage, the psychological toll of the conflict was crushing. 

> "I haven't the stamina to keep screaming inside for so long," Tove wrote in a letter to her close friend Eva Collan Korf. "I run to the studio and do some scrubbing or write verses... Newspapers, the radio, the phone—they churn up everything that's smoldering away deep inside."

Helsinki was subjected to devastating bombardment. Sirens pierced the night, glass shattered across cobblestone streets, and Tove spent endless hours shivering in overcrowded bomb shelters, waiting to hear if her brother was among the thousands of Finnish soldiers dying on the icy frontlines. The war, she later recalled, drained the world of vibrant hue; her oil paintings turned muddy, gray, and lifeless.

Seeking a retreat from her paralyzing depression, Tove reached back into her adolescent memory to a cheeky little figure she had drawn years earlier. 

The troll had originally been conceived when she was 15, born out of an old family joke. When Tove had once snuck into the kitchen for a late-night snack, her uncle warned her that "Moomintrolls"—frightening little creatures that lived behind the stove—would breathe down her neck if she stole food. In the 1940s, a stylized, skinny version of this creature began popping up in the corner of her *Garm* cartoons as her personal signature—an angry, mischievous little imp standing beside her name.

Now, as bombs fell on her city, Tove decided to build a story around him.

> "I suddenly felt an urge to write down something that was to begin with 'once upon a time,'" she remembered. "What followed had to be a fairy tale. That was inevitable. But I excused myself with avoiding princes, princesses, and small children, and chose instead my angry signature character and called him the Moomintroll."

## The Allegories of Displacement: *The Great Flood* and *The Comet*

Tove’s first Moomin book, *The Moomins and the Great Flood*, was penned during the dark winter of 1939–1940 and published in 1945. It is a striking document of wartime anxiety wrapped in a fairy tale. 

The narrative opens with Moomintroll and his mother wandering through a dark, menacing forest, searching desperately for Moominpappa, who has gone missing after marching off into the unknown. The landscape is alien and fraught with danger; thick yellow mists roll in, terrifying monsters lurk in the shadows, and a sudden catastrophic flood sweeps across the land, swallowing homes and separating families.

The real-world parallels were agonizingly sharp:
* **The Missing Father**: Reflected Tove’s own brother at the front and her traumatized father from 1918.
* **The Wandering Refugees**: Mirrored the real-life evacuation of over 70,000 Finnish children, who were tagged with identification cards and loaded onto trains to escape to Sweden, Norway, and Denmark.
* **Moominmamma’s Unshakeable Resolve**: Embodied Signe Hammarsten-Jansson, holding the domestic universe together while chaos raged outside.

```
+------------------------------------+------------------------------------+
| Real-World War Reality (1939-1945) | Moomin Mythos Allegory             |
+------------------------------------+------------------------------------+
| Soviet Air Raids & Shelling        | Approaching Fiery Comet            |
| Displaced Children & Refugees      | Wandering Trolls in Dark Forests   |
| Missing Soldiers on Frontlines     | Missing Moominpappa                |
| Devastation of Finnish Homeland    | The Great Flood Swallowing Valley  |
+------------------------------------+------------------------------------+
```

Tove followed this in 1946 with *Comet in Moominland*. In this second installment, a reddish sky hangs over Moominvalley as word spreads that a glowing comet is hurtling directly toward the Earth. Existential panic grips the forest. 

The characters react in ways that vividly capture human responses to impending doom:
* **Sniff** panics and runs away.
* **The Muskrat** lies lazily in a hammock reading bleak philosophy books, declaring that nothing matters anyway.
* **The Snork** attempts to bureaucracy his way out of disaster by chairing endless, pointless committee meetings.
* **The Observatory Scientists** sit atop a distant mountain, chain-smoking cigarettes and coldly calculating the exact second of impact (8:42 p.m., possibly 4 seconds later) without caring in the slightest about what happens to the living creatures below.

Yet, amid this atmospheric dread, Tove insisted on hope. Moominmamma packs a basket, making sure to bring along a frosted cake even as the sky turns fiery red. In Tove's world, holding on to simple, domestic pleasures—a cup of tea, a cozy cave, a slice of cake—was not foolish; it was the ultimate act of psychological resistance.

## A Cathedral of Light and International Triumph

The end of World War II brought a radical turning point in Tove Jansson's life. Following a catastrophic political argument at the family dinner table—where Tove and her father declared they hated each other—she packed up her easels, paints, and ration cards and moved out.

She secured the lease to a bombed-out rooftop studio in central Helsinki. The building was heavily damaged; wind howled through cracked walls, snow melted onto the floor, and the windows were completely blown out. Yet, for Tove, it was paradise. 

```
                       [ Tove's Rooftop Studio ]
                 - 12 Soaring Windows ("High as a Church")
                 - Wind, Cold drafts, Fractured Walls
                 - Complete Creative Independence
                                    |
                    +---------------+---------------+
                    |                               |
          [ Artistic Renaissance ]        [ Domestic Freedom ]
          - Vibrant oil painting          - Escape from patriarchal tensions
          - Writing Moomin Masterpieces   - Space for queer identity & female love
```

Standing in her freezing studio wrapped in a heavy winter coat and boots, gazing up at twelve massive windows that reached toward the light like a cathedral, she felt free for the first time in her life. She filled her space with color, sculptures, and draft pages.

In 1948, she published her third Moomin book, *Finn Family Moomintroll* (*Trollkarlens Hatt*). Unlike its bleak predecessors, this book was an effervescent celebration of summer, freedom, and low-stakes magic. The war was over, light had returned to the North, and readers across Europe were desperate for joy.

The book became an instant international sensation. Translated into dozens of languages, it launched "Moominmania." Within a few years, Tove was inundated with offers for:
* Daily comic strips in London's *Evening News* (reaching millions of readers daily)
* Merchandise contracts spanning scrapbooks, porcelain, greeting cards, and fabrics
* Radio plays, television adaptations, and theater productions

Tove found herself bewildered by the corporate machine. She joked in letters that journalists were "swarming like gray cats" and expressed frustration at how commercial management was pulling her away from painting. When Walt Disney himself approached her to buy the exclusive film and merchandising rights to the word "Moomin," Tove famously refused. She was determined to keep artistic control over her creation.

## The Psychological Depth of Moominvalley

What elevated the Moomin books far above standard children's literature was Tove's refusal to sanitize emotional complexity. Moominvalley was never a simple black-and-white morality play; it was a psychological ecosystem populated by deeply nuanced figures based on real people from Tove’s life:

### 1. Moominmamma
The ultimate anchor of security, modeled directly after Tove's mother, Ham. She never judges, never panics, and carries a handbag filled with dry socks, bark boats, and medicine for any emergency.

### 2. Moominpappa
Vain, nostalgic, and perpetually dreaming of grand adventure. He reflected Viktor Jansson's restless, artistic ego and his post-war struggle to find purpose in quiet domestic life.

### 3. Too-ticky
A wise, practical, and grounded craftswoman who sleeps in the Moomins' bathhouse during winter. She was modeled after Tuulikki Pietilä, a graphic artist who became Tove Jansson's lifelong romantic partner and companion for nearly 50 years.

### 4. The Groke (*Mörkö*)
Perhaps the most mysterious and tragic figure in the series. The Groke is a cold, ghostly monster who glides silently through the forest, freezing everything she touches. She is drawn to warmth and light, yet her very presence extinguishes flames and kills the grass. She is not evil; she is desperately lonely, representing lingering depression, grief, and unexpressed sorrow.

## Grief, Loss, and *Moominvalley in November*

By the late 1960s, Tove Jansson felt the Moomins had become a cage. "I'm becoming unable to escape any longer to the security of my secret cave," she wrote. "It's beginning to shut me in."

Then, in the summer of 1970, Signe Hammarsten-Jansson passed away. The loss of her mother tore away the central foundation of Tove's life. The family cottage on the archipelago felt alien and cold; the bright colors of summer vanished into grays and muted browns.

In her grief, Tove wrote her final Moomin novel: *Moominvalley in November* (1970). 

It remains one of the most remarkable narrative choices in modern literature. In this final book, **the Moomin family does not appear at all.**

Instead, a group of side characters—the anxious Fillyjonk, the lonely orphan Toft, the forgetful Grandpa-Grumble—all travel to the Moominhouse seeking the comfort, hot soup, and wisdom of Moominmamma. But when they arrive, they find the house shuttered, dark, and utterly empty. The family has vanished across the sea.

```
                      [ Moominvalley in November ]
                                   |
                  +----------------+----------------+
                  |                                 |
       [ Narrative Innovation ]                [ Emotional Subtext ]
    - Moomin family is absent              - Processing mother's death
    - Strangers occupy empty house         - Dismantling nostalgia & safety
    - House is cold, dark, silent          - Learning to accept grief & move on
```

The occupants are forced to live together in the chilly, empty house, navigating their own flaws and coming to terms with the fact that the sanctuary they were looking for cannot be given to them by someone else—it must be built within.

It was Tove's brave, heartbreaking farewell to her fictional sanctuary and to her mother. She never wrote another Moomin novel.

## The Enduring Legacy of Tove Jansson

Tove Jansson spent the remaining decades of her life on Klovharun, a remote, rocky islet in the Gulf of Finland. There, alongside Tuulikki Pietilä, she lived simply in a small wooden cabin surrounded by the sea, writing adult novels, painting, and corresponding with readers from all over the globe until her death in 2001 at the age of 86.

```
                             [ TOVE JANSSON ]
                       (August 1914 - June 2001)
                                    |
          +-------------------------+-------------------------+
          |                                                   |
[ The Universal Legacy ]                             [ The Artistic Ethos ]
- Over 50 Million Books Sold                         - Refusal to sanitize reality
- Cultural Icon of Finland                           - Honor both darkness & light
- Beloved by Generations Globally                    - Art as a sanctuary for survival
```

Today, the Moomins generate millions in global art installations, books, and screen adaptations. But their enduring power does not rest on clever marketing or cute character designs. 

The Moomins endure because Tove Jansson refused to lie to her readers, young or old. She understood that life is inherently precarious—that storms will roll in, comets will hover overhead, floods will wash away familiar paths, and cold specters will wander through the dark. 

Yet, by placing a glowing lantern in the window, brewing a warm pot of tea, and keeping the door open to lost wanderers, she showed the world that even in the deepest shadows of war and grief, joy is not just possible—it is an act of courage.

# 폭격의 잿더미에서 피어난 하얀 위로: '무민'의 숨겨진 전쟁사

동글동글한 하마를 닮은 체형, 순백의 피부, 몽글몽글한 눈망울. 핀란드를 대표하는 국민 캐릭터이자 전 세계 어린이들의 침대 곁을 지키는 '무민(Moomin)'을 보며 전쟁과 폭격을 떠올리는 이는 드물다. 서점 매대와 인테리어 소품, 전 세계 공항의 기념품 숍을 가득 채운 무민 트롤은 언제나 아늑하고 평화로운 동화의 상징이었다.

그러나 이 사랑스러운 캐릭터의 이면에는 20세기 가장 참혹했던 비극이 깊게 새겨져 있다. 머리 위로 소련군의 폭탄이 쏟아지던 지하실, 생사가 묘연한 가족을 향한 절망, 그리고 파시즘이 앗아간 일상 속에서 무민은 태어났다. 무민의 창작자 토베 얀손(Tove Jansson, 1914~2001)에게 무민 골짜기는 단순한 아동용 판타지가 아니라, 참혹한 전쟁의 공포로부터 자신을 지켜내기 위해 쌓아 올린 영혼의 방공호였다.

## 1. 전운(戰雲)이 드리운 북유럽, 붓 대신 펜을 든 예술가

1941년 6월 22일, 핀란드 헬싱키 동쪽 군도의 한 작은 오두막. 스물여섯 살의 화가 토베 얀손은 창밖의 바다를 바라보고 있었다. 구릿빛으로 물든 붉은 하늘 아래, 라디오에서는 청천벽력 같은 속보가 울려 퍼졌다. 나치 독일이 소련을 침공했다는 소식이었다. 

핀란드 사람들에게 이 소식의 의미는 명확했다. 얼마 지나지 않아 소련의 폭격기가 국경을 넘어올 것이라는 뜻이었다. 토베의 아버지는 짐을 싸며 덤덤하게 한마디를 던졌다. *"자, 올 것이 왔군."* 그 길로 가족들은 꽃이 만발한 여름 별장을 뒤로한 채 헬싱키행 피난선에 몸을 실었다.

토베 얀손은 뼛속까지 예술가 가문에서 자랐다. 저명한 조각가였던 아버지 빅토르 얀손(애칭 '파판')과 상업 일러스트레이터로 가계를 책임지던 스웨덴 출신의 어머니 시그네 함마르스텐 얀손(애칭 '함') 밑에서, 토베는 숨 쉬듯 그림을 배웠다. 14세에 이미 상업 잡지의 표지를 그렸을 만큼 조숙한 천재성을 보였지만, 그가 마주한 현실은 가혹했다.

갓 독립한 신생국 핀란드는 20세기 초반 피비린내 나는 내전을 겪었고, 제2차 세계대전이 발발하자 다시금 소련의 침공을 받았다(겨울전쟁과 계속전쟁). 약 400만 명의 작은 나라 핀란드는 생존을 위해 발버둥 쳐야 했다. 토베의 남동생은 전선으로 징집되었고, 매일같이 헬싱키 상공에서는 공습경보가 울렸다.

## 2. '성난 도깨비'에서 영혼의 안식처로

전쟁의 참상은 가족마저 갈라놓았다. 조국 수호에 광적으로 몰입했던 아버지는 극단적인 반공주의에 빠져 나치를 옹호하기에 이르렀다. 반면 토베에게 히틀러와 스탈린은 폭력이라는 동전의 양면에 불과했다. 정치적 견해 차이로 식탁에서 벌어진 말다툼 끝에 부녀는 *"서로를 증오한다"*는 극단적인 말까지 주고받았다. 

마음 둘 곳 없던 토베는 핀란드의 정치 풍자 잡지 《가름(Garm)》에 삽화를 기고하며 분노를 표출했다. 떼를 쓰는 아기 히틀러를 그리고, 스탈린의 이중성을 고발했다. 당시 검열의 칼날 속에서도 토베의 펜은 거침이 없었다. 

이 무렵, 그의 풍자화 구석 서명 옆에 기이하고 깡마른 생명체 하나가 불쑥 나타나기 시작했다. 뾰족한 코에 험상궂은 눈을 한 작은 트롤, 그가 훗날 '무민'이라 부르게 될 존재였다.

사실 무민은 어린 시절의 장난스러운 기억에서 출발했다. 십 대 시절 밤중에 몰래 주방을 털어 음식을 먹던 토베에게 삼촌은 *"밤에 돌아다니면 옷장 뒤에 숨어 있는 춥고 축축한 '무민트롤'이 나타난다"*며 겁을 주곤 했다. 그 오싹한 도깨비의 형상이 전쟁의 절망 속에서 다시 소환된 것이다.

하지만 토베의 펜 끝에서 트롤은 점차 변화하기 시작했다. 날카로운 모서리는 둥글어지고, 잿빛 형체는 눈처럼 새하얗게 채워졌다. 

> *"1938년, 폭격의 공포와 우울 속에서 음울한 생각들로부터 완전히 벗어나고 싶어 첫 번째 무민 이야기를 쓰기 시작했습니다. 모든 것이 자연스럽고, 선하며, 가능한 믿기지 않는 세계로 기어들어 갔죠. 왕자나 공주 대신 내 성난 시그니처 캐릭터를 택해 '무민트롤'이라 불렀습니다."*

## 3. 은유로 직조된 전쟁의 상처

전쟁 기간 토베 얀손이 쓴 초기 무민 연작은 동화의 외피를 쓴 '전쟁의 증언록'에 가깝다.

### ① 실향과 이별: 《무민 가족과 대홍수》(1945)
전쟁의 막바지에 집필된 첫 번째 책에서 무민트롤과 무민마마는 갑작스러운 대홍수로 집을 잃고 끝없는 어둠의 숲을 헤맨다. 그들의 목표는 실종된 아빠, '무민파파'를 찾는 것이다. 무민파파는 저 지평선 너머로 홀로 떠나 돌아오지 않고 있었다. 

이는 전선으로 떠난 남동생과 아버지의 무사 귀환을 애타게 기다리던 핀란드 여성들의 현실과 정확히 겹쳐진다. 실제로 당시 핀란드에서는 3만 명의 아이들이 아버지를 잃었고, 7만 명이 넘는 아동이 폭격을 피해 스웨덴 등지로 피난길에 올랐다.

### ② 하늘에서 떨어지는 불덩이: 《무민 골짜기에 나타난 혜성》(1946)
두 번째 이야기에서는 하늘에서 붉은 불꽃을 내뿜는 혜성이 무민 골짜기로 돌진한다. 지구가 산산조각 날 것이라는 공포 속에서 동물들은 패닉에 빠진다. 혜성은 당시 핀란드 상공을 가르던 소련의 폭탄, 나아가 인류를 공포에 떨게 한 원자폭탄의 명백한 은유였다.

주목할 점은 파국을 대하는 인물들의 태도다. 산꼭대기 천문대의 과학자들은 담배를 뻑뻑 피워대며 혜성이 충돌할 '정확한 초 단위의 시간'을 계산하는 데만 몰두한다. 반면 무민과 친구들은 살아남기 위해 행동한다. 동굴로 피신하면서도 무민마마는 소중한 케이크를 챙긴다. 멸망의 순간 직전에도 사람의 온기와 일상의 기쁨을 포기하지 않는 것, 이것이 바로 참호 속 핀란드인들이 버텨낸 방식이었다.

```
스웨덴어 격언 중에는 "Den som lever får se(살아남는 자가 보게 되리라)"라는 말이 있다.
토베 얀손의 동화는 죽음의 공포를 외면하지 않는다. 
다만 그 공포의 밑바닥에서 끝내 삶을 선택하는 법을 보여줄 뿐이다.
```

## 4. 폐허 속 옥탑방 스튜디오, 그리고 찾아온 해방

1944년 여름, 소련과의 휴전 협정이 체결되자 토베는 헬싱키 시내의 한 건물 꼭대기 층에 자리한 낡은 스튜디오를 얻어 독립했다. 전쟁 중 전사한 동료 화가가 쓰던 공간이었다. 

창문은 폭격으로 깨져나갔고, 벽체 곳곳이 무너져 내린 폐허였지만 토베는 그곳에 들어서는 순간 형언할 수 없는 행복을 느꼈다. 성당처럼 높게 솟은 천장과 빛이 쏟아져 들어오는 12개의 창문. 그는 외투를 껴입고 얼어붙은 손을 녹이며 미친 듯이 그림을 그리고 글을 썼다.

마침내 전쟁이 끝난 1948년, 세 번째 책 《마법사가 잃어버린 모자(Finn Family Moomintroll)》가 출간되었다. 앞선 두 권의 암울한 재난 서사에서 벗어나, 찬란한 여름날의 모험과 따스한 자유를 노래한 이 작품은 출간 즉시 전 세계적인 돌풍을 일으켰다.

영국 언론에 만화가 연재되었고, 전 세계 수십 개국에 번역 출간되었다. 머그잔, 인형, 직물 등 캐릭터 상품 계약이 쇄도했다. 심지어 미국의 거물 월트 디즈니(Walt Disney)가 찾아와 '무민'이라는 명칭의 독점 사용권을 사겠다고 제안했으나, 토베는 단칼에 거절했다. 자신의 상처와 치유가 담긴 분신을 상업 거인의 손에 넘길 수는 없었기 때문이다.

## 5. 슬픔을 건너온 이들에게 건네는 안식

토베 얀손의 무민 시리즈는 1970년 출간된 여덟 번째 소설 《11월의 무민 골짜기(Moominvalley in November)》를 끝으로 긴 여정을 마쳤다. 

평생 예술적 동지이자 가장 깊은 유대를 나누었던 어머니 '함'의 죽음 앞에서 쓰인 이 마지막 작품에는 놀랍게도 무민 가족이 한 명도 등장하지 않는다. 주인이 떠나 텅 빈 무민의 집을 찾아온 이웃들이 차가운 빈방에서 각자의 슬픔과 상실을 마주하며 성장해 나가는 이야기다. 

그것은 어머니를 떠나보낸 토베 자신의 처절한 애도이자, 독자들에게 건네는 성숙한 작별 인사였다.

## 삶이라는 거친 파도를 건너는 법

현대인들이 무민 골짜기에서 발견하는 것은 단순한 귀여움이 아니다. 무민의 세계에는 언제나 차가운 바람을 몰고 오는 유령 '그로크'가 배회하고, 불평불만 많은 '미이'가 쏘아붙이며, 언제 터질지 모르는 재난의 그림자가 짙게 깔려 있다. 

그러나 무민 골짜기의 주민들은 두려움 때문에 오늘의 삶을 멈추지 않는다. 혜성이 떨어져도 동굴 안에서 다 함께 찌그러진 케이크를 나누어 먹고, 폭풍우가 지나간 바닷가에서 아름다운 조개껍데기를 줍는다.

토베 얀손은 비극을 지우는 대신, 비극을 감싸 안는 법을 택했다. 제2차 세계대전이라는 인류사적 지옥 속에서 그가 건져 올린 순백의 트롤은, 80여 년이 지난 지금도 불확실하고 위태로운 세계를 살아가는 우리에게 조용히 속삭이고 있다.

**"비록 세상이 무너질지라도, 우리는 오늘 우리의 차를 마시고 삶을 계속해야 해."**
