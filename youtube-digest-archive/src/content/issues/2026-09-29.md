# The Digital Palate: How the Internet is Redefining Our Relationship with Food

### Episode summary

The internet is profoundly shaping our food culture, leading to a "sameness" in online recipes characterized by "creamy, jammy, lemony" adjectives and globally sourced "flavor bombs" like miso and gochujang. This shift, driven by recipes needing to "fend for themselves" online, transforms recipe developers into marketers, prioritizing catchy descriptions over traditional culinary guidance, exemplified by the viral "beany leaky greens with Greeky rampy beans" tweet.

This algorithmic influence extends to a "silly little drinks era" where wellness culture promotes consuming more products (like fiber sodas or protein-packed ice creams) under the guise of health, echoing historical "wellness drinks" like early Coca-Cola. Viewers should critically examine the "highly industrialized, highly processed food products" presented as healthy, recognizing that current food trends are often a complex interplay of convenience, marketing, and a desire for specific nutritional numbers, rather than purely traditional or simple eating.

Remember that viral tweet from comedian Carly Maruli in 2024? The one where a desperate cook asks, "What should I do?" and the New York Times Cooking section replies, "Beanie leaky greens with Greeky rampy beans." If you’ve seen it, you’re not alone. This short, humorous exchange has resonated with countless people because it perfectly captures a pervasive feeling: online recipes often feel… eerily similar. They’re saturated with adjectives, feature globally sourced "flavor bombs," and are accompanied by photos so luscious they border on the erotic.

But how did we get here? What has the internet done to our palates, and what do our contemporary tastes say about us? To explore these questions, we turn to Ruby Tando, a distinguished cookbook writer and author of *All Consuming: Why We Eat the Way We Eat Now*.

### The Algorithmic Recipe Phenomenon

[[SUM]] Online recipes have converged into a sensory-heavy "sameness" using "y"-ending adjectives and global flavor bombs to compete for attention in a digital marketplace where every dish must market itself.

[[AT:H2jY1DFNl7c:215]]

The "beanie leaky greens" tweet isn't just a joke; it’s a sharp observation about the current state of online food culture. Tando notes that recipes online seem to have "converged on a kind of sameness that's hard to escape." They are described with a litany of "y"-ending words – creamy, jammy, lemony – and frequently incorporate ingredients like miso, gochujang, and harissa.

This phenomenon, Tando argues, has a clear provenance. Traditionally, we learned to cook from cookbooks, where each recipe was allowed to stand on its own. The hard work of marketing a specific dish was done by the cookbook itself, relying on the author's reputation. "I'm no longer putting my trust in Martha Stewart," Tando explains. Instead, online, "every recipe has to fend for itself." In a sea of millions of recipes, each one needs to leap out, leading to "more adjectives, more kind of grabby sensory descriptions." The role of a recipe developer has evolved from merely creating a good recipe to actively marketing it. The most successful content creators today are those adept at marketing relatively simple dishes in clever ways.

This shift is evident when comparing culinary influences. A generation ago, one might trust a chef like Nigel Slater implicitly, cooking unfamiliar fish like mackerel simply because he endorsed it. Today, a new voice like Allison Roman might gain traction with "the stew" or "the shallot pasta," but her status as a trusted voice is often contingent on stringing together enough "superstar recipes."

### The Social Fabric of Taste

[[SUM]] Culinary preferences are primarily social transactions used to define group belonging or rebellion, rather than innate traits, as our environment and social identity profoundly dictate what we choose to eat.

[[AT:H2jY1DFNl7c:123]]

Beyond individual preferences, Tando emphasizes that "all taste is prescribed socially." While some innate likes and dislikes exist (like the genetic predisposition to find cilantro soapy), most of our culinary preferences are learned and influenced by our surroundings. Taste, she posits, is a "transaction," deeply intertwined with "which groups you want to be included in and which you definitely don't want to be a part of." From childhood rebellions against family dinners to adult contrarian streaks, our social environment profoundly shapes our hunger and our choices.

### Globalization and "Twist Culture"

[[SUM]] Creators use "twist culture" by adding concentrated ingredients like miso or gochujang to simple dishes, prioritizing differentiation in a crowded digital field over traditional culinary boundaries or complex spice blending.

[[AT:H2jY1DFNl7c:401]]

The internet has not only changed *how* recipes are presented but also *what* ingredients are used. Tando finds it charmingly ironic that ingredients like gochujang, once painstakingly sourced by Korean grandmothers, are now readily available in Caribbean neighborhoods in America and frequently incorporated into dishes like "creamy gochujang leak sausage pasta."

This mixing and matching of ingredients across cultures is driven by a desire for simplicity. Instead of buying a dozen dry spices to create a complex blend, many home cooks opt for a single "flavor bomb" – a miso, a harissa, a gochujang – to add depth. As UK recipe developer Easter Belfridge aptly calls them, these are concentrated bursts of taste.

However, the primary impetus for this "twist culture" isn't solely deliciousness. In the crowded online recipe ecosystem, creators must do something to set themselves apart. Adding miso to an unexpected dish is often a way to differentiate. While Tando acknowledges that this blending frequently leads to "fantastic cooking" and "more often than not, it is delicious," the original drive was often less about culinary perfection and more about standing out in an "incredibly busy field." Fusion food, once a distinct category, has now become the default, with cooks constantly crossing culinary boundaries.

### The Americanization of Global Tastes

[[SUM]] American food culture exerts a hegemonic influence globally, particularly in the UK, where social media and viral videos drive a deep, often unconscious fascination with American-style dining and products.

[[AT:H2jY1DFNl7c:618]]

Despite the global reach of the internet, Tando makes a striking observation: online culture is primarily American culture. For instance, in the UK, food trends promoted on TikTok and Instagram are often American-centric, featuring "Guy Fieri flavor town maxing burgers" or American-style barbecue. Tando notes a "fundamental disconnect" between how much Brits absorb American food culture, often unconsciously, and the American perception of its global influence. From films to social media, the United States has profoundly shaped British food culture since the 1950s, far more than neighboring European countries or even its own diaspora communities. This fascination is reciprocal, with popular YouTube videos featuring "British school kids trying like Chick-fil-A for the very first time" garnering millions of views, catering to both British curiosity and American interest in reactions to their cuisine.

### The "Silly Little Drinks" Era and Wellness Culture

[[SUM]] Modern wellness culture promotes consuming more products through "silly little drinks" like fiber sodas and functional waters, echoing historical marketing strategies that once framed early Coca-Cola as a medicinal tonic.

[[AT:H2jY1DFNl7c:803]]

Our preoccupation with food extends beyond solid meals to a burgeoning "silly little drinks era." From kombucha and bubble tea to fiber sodas and magnesium water, we are in a "highly liquid food culture." It’s not uncommon to see people, including the host, simultaneously sipping on two or three different beverages, each promising a unique benefit.

Tando points out that what seems like a modern phenomenon has historical roots. When Starbucks first emerged decades ago, the idea of a Frappuccino seemed outlandish to some. Now, the variety of "silly little drinks" has exploded far beyond anything imaginable. This trend, Tando suggests, is a triumph of wellness culture, which encourages us to "streamline your diet and to kind of eat healthy in some cases to eat less, but somehow by consuming more products than ever before." Drinks become the perfect vehicle for this, offering perceived health benefits – more fiber, better workouts, reduced bloating – often at a premium price.

This isn't new. In the late 1800s, iconic beverages like Coca-Cola and Pepsi were originally marketed as "wellness drinks," promising cures for headaches and stomach ailments, some even containing actual drugs. Today's "ceremonial matcha" from a chain coffee shop or a "Hayley Bieber air-worn smoothie" echo these historical marketing strategies, tapping into our desire for quick fixes and added benefits.

This constant pursuit of "healthy" has led to an "orthorexic moment," an obsession with "clean" or "healthy" eating, heavily driven by algorithms. Online videos push protein-maxing (cottage cheese in everything) or fiber-maxing (a trend the host admits to appreciating). Amidst this "noise," an "ideological war" rages over what constitutes healthy eating, making it incredibly difficult to discern what's genuinely beneficial. Generic advice like "get in your five a day" now feels "so generic as to be useless," replaced by a demand for numbers, products, and definitive figures to alleviate confusion.

### Frankenfoods and the Industrial Palate

[[SUM]] The demand for specific nutritional metrics has birthed "Frankenfoods"—highly processed, industrially engineered products like protein ice cream designed by venture-backed forecasting industries to meet modern dietary obsessions with convenience.

[[AT:H2jY1DFNl7c:1173]]

This demand for specific nutritional metrics has fueled the rise of "Frankenfoods" – industrially processed products designed to hit specific macros while offering convenience. An article in *The Atlantic* highlighted protein ice cream with 210 calories and 30 grams of protein per pint, made with a "protein system," a "fat system," and a "flavor system" – terms that sound more like engineering than culinary art. Similarly, influencers promote "clear drinkable protein lemonade" powders, raising questions about what these highly processed concoctions truly are.

Tando explains that this innovation is turbocharged by massive investment and a "weird forecasting industry." Food expos become arenas where buyers, manufacturers, and investors decide the next big thing – be it fake meat or a boba tea machine. Venture capitalists pour money into product development, leading to rapid innovation that once took years now happening in months. These companies are, in essence, delivering "exactly what we want based on our broken food culture" – high protein, low calorie, delicious, probiotic, high fiber, all with minimal prep.

This isn't the first time industrial food has met cultural demand. The 1950s saw a similar tension, with figures like Julia Child championing "real food from real cultures" as a reaction against the rise of microwave meals and processed foods. While this movement gave us beautiful cuisine, it also denied the reality that factory-made foods were providing "mass nourishment" in a post-war era. Today, we face a similar choice: embrace a romanticized view of traditional food or be a realist about the need for processed solutions to integrate nutrients and provide convenience.

### The Performance of Homemaking and the Complication of Cooking

[[SUM]] Cooking has transitioned from a basic household chore into a performative act of self-expression and class signaling, where the internet turns simple nourishment into a complex, aspirational, and often unnecessarily difficult discourse.

[[AT:H2jY1DFNl7c:1481]]

Our relationship with food has also been complicated by shifts in the perception of homemaking. In the 1970s and '80s, as feminism challenged traditional homemaking expectations, magazines like *Gourmet* and *Bon Appétit* became popular, shifting the focus from feeding a household to "learning, travel, self-improvement, hobbyism, entertaining, and impressing people." This era, alongside "yuppie culture," birthed the term "foodie," which Tando notes came into common parlance around the same time. "Foodie" culture made eating "far more about participating in discourse and active kind of self-definition," transforming a fundamental need into a complex act of self-expression and class signaling.

Now, while "homemaking" is making a comeback online, often in the form of "tradwife content," it too is largely performative. Tando starkly contrasts this with genuine homemaking: "When your back is against the wall, you don't think about what kind of homemaker you are. You just do things. The work is what it is." For the camera, however, it's about "what kind of homemaker you are, what your brand is." This leads to absurdities like making Oreos from scratch when a store-bought option is readily available, making cooking "way harder than it needs to be" and imbuing it with layers of political and personal meaning.

Ultimately, Tando concludes that the complication of cooking is "a problem of our own creation." For those with basic stability and a functional kitchen, "the question of feeding yourself is really very simple. Everything else, the discourse is what we have made to make it more difficult." We are trapped in a "prison" where every recipe must be the "best imaginable version," and the internet, with its endless options and performative demands, only tightens the bars.

From the humorous simplicity of "beanie leaky greens" to the complex industrial processes behind "protein systems," our digital world is undeniably reshaping what we eat, how we cook, and what we value in food. It's a landscape where convenience, wellness, and self-expression intertwine, challenging us to discern genuine nourishment from algorithmic influence.

# When Healing Harms: The Perils of Public Trauma in Modern Society

### Episode summary

Katherine Liu, a cultural theorist, argues that modern "trauma culture" commodifies personal suffering, transforming it into a "trauma script" that undermines genuine political action and intellectual debate. She traces its origins to late 19th-century "railway spine" diagnoses for wealthy businessmen, contrasting this with the unacknowledged, systemic suffering of industrial workers. Liu criticizes figures like AOC and Oprah for modeling this script, turning personal narratives of sexual assault or childhood abuse into public spectacles that, while expanding permissible discourse, ultimately distract from deeper societal issues.

Liu, a Marxist, contends this focus on individual trauma, often by the professional-managerial class, eclipses class conflict and the economic struggles of the majority. She urges viewers to recognize how this "content farming machine" provides an "escape from reality," preventing necessary debates about a just society. While acknowledging the desire to make meaning from suffering, Liu suggests that authentic community bonds, rather than public performance, are crucial for processing trauma and fostering genuine social change.

On college campuses, a curious phenomenon has taken root: the personal essay, once a canvas for diverse narratives, has increasingly narrowed its scope to the "trauma story." As students at the Harvard Crimson discovered, this expectation to frame personal suffering as a credential for recognition can be deeply unsettling. When young people are encouraged to "frame your trauma, share it, and make it a credential," they risk objectifying a part of themselves, alienating themselves from their own lived experience. This observation, from cultural theorist and professor Katherine Liu, cuts to the heart of a pervasive issue in contemporary society: the commodification of trauma and its far-reaching implications for individuals, intellectual life, and democracy itself.

Liu, author of the scathing new book *Traumatized: The New Politics of Public Suffering*, argues that this "commodified trauma script" – the careless, constant sharing of personal wounds – is not only detrimental to our individual well-being but also corrosive to robust public discourse. From the hallowed halls of academia to the sprawling landscape of social media, our modern understanding of trauma, its public expression, and its purported healing mechanisms, she contends, has become deeply problematic, often serving to eclipse genuine political action with performance.

## The Curious Case of Railway Spine: Tracing Trauma's Origins

[[SUM]] The modern trauma script originated in the late 19th century with "railway spine," a diagnosis created to help wealthy businessmen sue railway companies for psychological shock.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:126]]

To understand the modern "trauma script," Liu takes us back to its surprising origins in the late 19th century. Our contemporary ideas of trauma, she explains, are intimately linked to a diagnosis that emerged from the terrifying new technology of the British rail system. Businessmen traveling between London and the north, unaccustomed to such speed and the inherent risks of railway accidents, would often return home profoundly shaken, unable to resume their professional lives.

These men, though often physically unwounded, sought to sue railway companies for damages. This led to a medical and legal conundrum, culminating in the diagnosis of "railway spine." Doctors of the era believed the shaking of the trains concussed these men, leading to a "neurosthenic shock" that manifested in symptoms we might now recognize as flashbacks or an inability to function. The railway companies, seeking to limit liability, inadvertently spurred the invention of psychotherapy. Their solution: tell these men to see their doctors and talk about their issues, implying a psychological rather than purely physical malady.

This marked a pivotal shift. Trauma, as a medical diagnosis, was born out of a very specific notion of damage and shock to the body of a *wealthy, competent, professional man*. It was about monetizing risk and liability for a powerful new technology, providing a pathway for affluent individuals to seek medical treatment and sue for damages even without visible wounds.

## When Suffering Became Secular: A Tale of Two Traumas

[[SUM]] Unlike pre-modern societies that viewed suffering as an inevitable, communitarian experience with religious meaning, modern secularized trauma treats suffering as a personal psychological affliction that can be financially repaired.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:284]]

But surely, suffering and injury occurred long before the advent of the railroad? Liu acknowledges this, contrasting pre-modern understandings of affliction with the secularized, medicalized trauma of today. In earlier civilizations, suffering, pain, or a "blow of fate" were imbued with social and religious meaning – perhaps a punishment from the gods, or an unavoidable part of the human condition. The "inner life" was not typically seen as something that could be "repaired" through treatment; rather, it was something to be endured, with endurance itself holding high cultural value, particularly in communitarian societies.

The modern concept of "recovery" and "treatment" for trauma only truly arises when it is secularized, given meaning through medical and legal institutions. Liu offers a compelling example of this psychologization of history through Christopher Nolan's imagined adaptation of Homer's *Odyssey*. In the ancient epic, Odysseus's trials are attributed to the anger of the gods. Nolan, Liu posits, would likely reframe this as Odysseus suffering from PTSD due to his actions in Troy, needing to "work it through" with a therapist-like figure (perhaps a "yoga teacher Bali new age healer" instead of a seductive goddess). The powerful, desiring goddess Circe, from an oral history thousands of years old, would be reduced to a therapist.

This illustrates a crucial distinction: in the modern trauma script, suffering becomes a personal, psychological affliction that can be fixed by paying someone to "repair" it. It allows even those who are part of a "winning group" – the wealthy and powerful – to identify themselves as suffering individuals, deserving of unique attention and care.

## The Class Component: Trauma as a Humanizer for the Privileged

[[SUM]] Modern trauma discourse largely serves to humanize the wealthy while ignoring the systemic, unmonetized physical and mental injuries endured by the working class.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:476]]

This brings us to Liu's central Marxist critique: trauma, as it is largely discussed today, serves to "humanize the wealthy and the people with resources while kind of hiding the real injury of people who are perhaps working-class." She points to the industrial revolution, where countless workers dropped dead in front of looms or toiled 16-hour days in factories, often nameless Irish migrants escaping famine. Their experiences, though profoundly damaging to body and mind, were not framed as "traumatic" in the medical-legal sense; they were simply "working-class life."

"Trauma never is talked about with regard to the working class," Liu asserts. Instead, it emerges for the "gentle lawyer businessman" who suffers a railway accident. Their work capabilities become monetized, a question of investment and liability. When a factory worker died, another was simply put in their place. The long-term, damaging effects of stress from low wages and poor treatment – conditions still prevalent today – are rarely discussed in terms of trauma because, for these individuals, there is no "salable recovery." They cannot afford a $5,000 healing retreat.

Liu, identifying as a Marxist, explains her approach: she looks for the class component, examining how ideas are ideologically bound to notions of late Cold War or post-Cold War liberalism. Her work, she says, is a "historical materialist ideological critique," one that prioritizes class analysis over the "race, class, narrative with race and colonialism identity politics first" approach currently popular in academia.

## Identity Pluralism and the Eclipse of Conflict

[[SUM]] American identity pluralism reduces politics to the mutual recognition of personal suffering, eclipsing the robust Marxist reality of class conflict and the need for wealth redistribution.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:734]]

This Marxist lens is crucial to understanding Liu's critique of "identity pluralism." This American liberal ideal envisions a society where diverse religions, races, and ethnicities live in harmony, achieved by continually "unfolding new identities into the polity and recognizing these new identities." Trauma culture, Liu argues, folds neatly into this, reducing the political sphere to mere "mutual recognition" of each other's suffering.

What this framework overlooks, according to Liu, is the more robust "high liberalism" that recognized debate, dissent, and contradiction – concepts rooted in Marx. The interests of the capitalist and the worker, she emphasizes, are not merely "different identities"; they represent "different relations to the means of the ownership of the means of production." While identity pluralism envisions a "thousand points of light" harmony, Marxism posits a "brutal conflict" between classes, where a just society would involve a redistribution of surplus value.

Around the 1960s and '70s, Liu notes, people of color and women began to be uniquely positioned as "traumatized." The public sphere was envisioned as transformed if these individuals "testified to our particular trauma," leading white liberals to exclaim, "Oh my god, this is so horrible," and everyone would then be "happy." This, she suggests, replaces genuine structural critique with a focus on personal narrative.

## The Celebrity Spectacle: When Personal Revelation Becomes Political Performance

[[SUM]] Public figures like Oprah, Prince Harry, and AOC model a commodified trauma script that turns personal suffering into a brand, which ultimately closes down serious political debate.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:1077]]

Liu is particularly critical of public figures who deploy the trauma script. While acknowledging that all humans experience "blows of fate," she argues that "educated rich people" control the media, and their suffering is framed in a way that audiences are supposed to emulate. Figures like Oprah Winfrey and Prince Harry model the trauma script, turning their personal struggles into public service announcements and, ultimately, brands. Prince Harry, for instance, has openly discussed his trauma from losing his mother and the paparazzi, detailing his use of EMDR therapy and making his "recovery" his brand in Hollywood.

Even progressive figures like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez come under scrutiny. Liu recalls AOC's social media stream after the January 6th attacks, where instead of focusing solely on the political implications, she connected hiding in her office to past trauma from sexual assault. Liu admits a "maternal feeling" of "Don't do this. Don't do this online," but recognizes it as a millennial tendency.

While Liu concedes that such revelations expand "what is permissible to speak about in public" – a "net positive" that helps normalize conversations about sexual assault or abuse, previously hidden or disbelieved – she argues there's a significant downside. This deep saturation of the political sphere with "personal experience, what we like to call lived experience," can "close down serious debate."

The shift is stark: from Thomas Eagleton being dropped as a vice-presidential candidate in the 1970s for having received electroshock therapy (mental illness being an unspoken taboo in public life), to a present where "everyone's crazy in Washington and it seems to be fine." While the expansion of public discourse around women's suffering is positive, Liu worries about a corresponding "greater expansion of just acting out with irrationality" among those in high office.

She also critiques the "imperative to talk about oneself," particularly among professional women. Discussions about menopause in the workplace or AOC publicly documenting her egg-freezing process, while seemingly empowering, often cater to a "PMC feminist idea of choice." This spectacle, Liu argues, primarily benefits "professional women making huge salaries" who can afford such choices, doing little to help vulnerable, working-class women struggling with childcare costs or medical bills. It becomes a "class-based, celebrity-based spectacle exercise."

## The Cost to Democracy: Escapism and the Erosion of Debate

[[SUM]] The constant public performance of personal trauma acts as an escape from deteriorating socio-economic realities, leaving citizens too emotionally immobilized to engage in rational democratic debate.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:1484]]

Liu challenges the notion that public trauma sharing is inherently revolutionary. While acknowledging a desire for attention and meaning-making, she suggests that the push to play out trauma in public might stem from the undermining of authentic social bonds by social media, the pandemic, and the atomization of competitive capitalism. This public performance, she contends, often fails to help individuals truly process their trauma, instead providing "fodder for other people." It creates a dangerous illusion where "revelation of our personal selves" is mistaken for "revolution."

For those who are content with capitalism or the status quo, Liu issues a stark warning: even if you like capitalism, the current form is failing the majority. Statistics reveal that a significant percentage of Americans struggle to pay rent, food, and medical bills, leading to cuts in essential medication or deeper credit card debt. This, she argues, is "not a good form of capitalism."

The constant focus on personal trauma and celebrity culture, Liu believes, acts as an "escape from these intractable issues we think we cannot fix." We are "propagandized into thinking our escapes, the escape from the reality, is somehow going to make up for a deteriorating social and economic life for the majority of people." This "escape into the self" or "esoteric forms of theory" prevents the necessary debates about what a just society should look like.

A healthy democracy, Liu contends, relies on education, rational argument, and a powerful fourth estate – all of which are undermined by "gangster capitalism" and an "effectual content farming machine that just sucks the lifeblood out of us and regurgitates it for us for our consumption." This environment breeds "affectually immobilized people" who are easily triggered, unable to engage in serious discussions about "third rail topics" like immigration or gender. Critical voices, particularly on the liberal left, are often "mobbed online," a phenomenon Liu links not to organic movements but to "donors, NGOs, funders, wealthy leaders and the democratic party."

## The Sacred Survivor and the Silencing of Dissent

[[SUM]] The modern "survivor identity" positions suffering as a sacred credential, which is used to shut down intellectual disagreement and demonize critics as lacking empathy.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:1826]]

Liu also critiques the "survivor identity," which she sees as a foundational concept of modern identitarianism. Building on the work of feminists like Judith Herman and anti-war advocates like Robert Jay Lifton, she suggests a "crypto-religious positioning of the survivor." The survivor becomes a "sacred character," privileged because they have endured something horrible, thus making them "more politically and emotionally spiritually attuned to what is wrong with the world."

Fifty years later, this has evolved into a belief that "there's something sacred about my suffering, about my lived experience which will make me uniquely positioned to comment on the state of the world or the state of my university or the state of the discipline." The dangerous consequence: "If you criticize me, then you're just showing lack of compassion, lack of empathy, and I can shut you down and demonize you." This stifles "vigorous debate," a cornerstone of intellectual life and democracy. The ability to argue, agree, disagree, and remain respectful, Liu warns, is being lost, and "if we don't have that ability, our democracy is done for."

## From Feeling to Thinking: Reclaiming Authenticity and Debate

[[SUM]] To reclaim a healthy democracy, individuals must reserve trauma processing for private relationships and transition from emotional, public self-objectification back to rigorous, historical thinking.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:1981]]

So, what is the appropriate way to think about and utilize trauma? Liu acknowledges that trauma is a "real phenomenon" – we all suffer "blows of fate for which we are not prepared," and physical violations or paralyzing experiences need to be treated with care. The "cortisol reaction," the fight-or-flight response, is real.

However, she stresses that the management of such experiences and the journey towards self-knowledge "don't have to be shared all the time with everyone." Trauma should not be a "legitimizing credential." The "self-objectification" that our culture demands through the public sharing of trauma, she argues, has a damaging effect that we haven't fully measured. True authenticity and empathy, Liu believes, should be reserved for those close to us and for our personal journey of self-knowledge, not a "commodified trauma script that we're just like carelessly sharing all the time." Such a script, she concludes, is "not good for ourselves and I don't think it's good for democracy. I don't think it's good for debate, dissent, intellectual life."

What should replace the incessant discussions of trauma and personal suffering in public debates? Liu advocates for "a more rigorous historical understanding of how we got where we are today." We also need a collective "reckoning with ourselves and our desires for escapism." She confesses her own addiction to dog rescue videos on Instagram, recognizing it as a form of "theater of fake empathy" that social media provides, allowing her to escape the helplessness she feels about the world's horrors.

Resisting this "content farming machine" is crucial, Liu argues, "in order to be better democratic citizens and in order to be to participate more seriously in political debates about what our country should look like, what our future should look like." This means moving beyond being "easily triggered, kind of effectually immobilized people" on sensitive topics. We need to respect the "craft and culture of thinking itself," rejecting "easy solutions, easy way outs," and "volunteeristic gestural things like 'I'll just decolonize the curriculum,'" which she sees as doing nothing.

Ultimately, the call is clear: we need to thoughtfully understand history, respect the past, and look together towards a collective future. We need to move "from just feeling to going back to thinking." And as Liu aptly notes, with a wry chuckle, "Thinking is hard." Indeed, but it is precisely this difficult, critical engagement that, she argues, offers the only genuine path to a more just and democratic society.

# 알고리즘의 만찬: 인터넷이 바꾼 우리의 식탁 풍경

### 에피소드 요약

온라인에서 레시피를 검색해 본 사람이라면 누구나 한 번쯤 겪어봤을 법한 풍경이 있습니다. "크리미하고 쫀득하며 레몬 향 가득한 그린 램피 빈 수프" 같은 과도한 수식어와 특정 재료의 조합이 난무하는 레시피들 말이죠. 2024년 코미디언 칼리 마룰리(Carly Maruli)가 올린 "Beanie leaky greens with Greeky rampy beans"라는 트윗은 이러한 온라인 식문화의 단면을 유머러스하게 포착하며 수많은 이들의 공감을 얻었습니다.

NPR 팟캐스트 '잇츠 빈 어 미닛(It's Been a Minute)'은 요리책 작가이자 '모든 것을 소비하다: 우리가 지금 먹는 방식, 그 이유(All Consuming: Why We Eat the Way We Eat Now)'의 저자인 루비 탄도(Ruby Tandoh)를 초대하여, 인터넷이 우리의 미각과 식탁을 어떻게 변화시켰는지, 그리고 오늘날의 음식 취향이 우리 자신에 대해 무엇을 말해주는지 심층적으로 탐구했습니다.

## 알고리즘이 빚어낸 '비슷한 맛': 온라인 레시피의 진화

[[SUM]] 온라인 레시피는 알고리즘 노출을 위해 '크리미' 등 자극적 형용사와 미소·고추장 같은 '맛 폭탄' 재료를 활용해 스스로를 마케팅하며 맛의 획일화를 초래한다.

탄도 작가는 "건강에 대한 이야기는 더 이상 고립적으로 나타나지 않는다"며, 앳킨스 다이어트(Atkins diet)의 실패나 육식주의(carnivore movement)와 같은 특정 식단이 채식주의나 식물 기반 문화에 대한 정치적 반응으로 나타난다고 설명합니다. 이제 사람들은 막연한 '좋은 식습관' 대신 구체적인 숫자, 제품, 그리고 자신들의 혼란을 해소해 줄 특정 인물을 원합니다.

이러한 욕구는 '프랑켄푸드(frankenfoods)'라고 불리는 고도로 가공된 식품의 등장을 촉진합니다. '애틀랜틱(The Atlantic)' 기사에 따르면, 단백질 바 회사인 데이비드(David)는 210칼로리에 단백질 30g을 함유한 아이스크림을 만드는데, 이는 "단백질 시스템(protein system)", "지방 시스템(fat system)", "맛 시스템(flavor system)"으로 구성됩니다. 이는 아이스크림처럼 보이지만 영양 성분은 전혀 다른, 고도로 산업적으로 가공된 제품입니다. 물에 타면 투명한 단백질 레모네이드가 되는 제품처럼, 사람들은 고단백, 저칼로리, 맛있고, 프로바이오틱스, 고섬유질 등의 모든 것을 한 번에 충족시키는 '편의식품'을 원하며, 공장들은 이러한 수요에 맞춰 초고속으로 제품을 개발합니다.

하지만 이러한 현상에도 역사적 맥락이 있습니다. 1950년대에 줄리아 차일드(Julia Child)와 같은 요리 작가들이 이탈리아, 프랑스, 인도 등 다양한 문화권의 '진짜 음식(real food)'을 강조하며 등장한 것은 당시 확산되던 산업화된 가공식품에 대한 반작용이었습니다. 그들은 전자레인지 식사 대신 음식에 '낭만'을 불어넣으려 했습니다. 오늘날 우리는 다시 한번 이러한 기로에 서 있습니다. '아이스크림이 아이스크림이던 시절'을 그리워하는 낭만주의자가 될 것인가, 아니면 대량 영양 공급과 기능성 향상을 위해 가공식품의 필요성을 인정하는 현실주의자가 될 것인가 하는 질문에 직면해 있습니다.

## 요리는 '자기 표현'인가, '생존'인가?: 가정식의 정치학

탄도 작가는 가정 생활(homemaking)과 음식 콘텐츠가 분리되었던 역사를 짚습니다. 1970년대와 80년대 페미니즘 운동이 가정 주부의 역할에 의문을 제기하면서, '고메(Gourmet)'나 '보나페티(Bon Appetit)' 같은 잡지들은 요리를 '배움, 여행, 자기 계발, 취미, 접대, 사람들에게 깊은 인상을 주는 것'으로 묘사하기 시작했습니다. 이는 '가족이나 자신을 먹이는 것'과는 거리가 먼, 계층과 취향을 표현하는 수단이 된 것입니다.

최근에는 '가정 생활'과 '요리'가 다시 합쳐지는 듯한 모습도 보이지만, 이는 종종 '트래디 와이프(tradwife)' 콘텐츠처럼 카메라를 위한 '수행적인(performative)' 행위에 가깝습니다. 탄도 작가는 "진정한 가정 생활은 카메라를 위한 것이 아닐 때, 등 떠밀려 할 때는 어떤 종류의 주부인지 생각할 틈이 없다. 그저 해야 할 일일 뿐"이라고 말합니다. 반면 카메라 앞에서 오레오를 직접 만드는 노라 스미스(Nora Smith)의 사례처럼, 오늘날의 요리는 스스로를 먹여 살리는 데 필요한 것보다 훨씬 더 복잡하고 정치적인 의미를 담게 되었습니다.

탄도 작가는 음식을 통한 '담론 참여와 적극적인 자기 정의'가 요리를 이토록 복잡하게 만들었다고 결론 내립니다. 기본적인 의식주가 해결된 상황에서 자신을 먹이는 문제는 사실 매우 단순하지만, 우리가 만들어낸 수많은 담론이 그 과정을 어렵게 만들었다는 것입니다. 여기에 '가족을 먹이고 가정을 꾸리는 것'이라는 과거의 이상적인 가정 생활의 요소까지 다시금 주입되면서, 요리는 더욱 복잡하고 이상적인 '푸디 문화'의 감옥에 갇히게 되었습니다.

루비 탄도 작가와의 대화는 인터넷이 우리의 식탁을 단순히 편리하게 만든 것을 넘어, 우리의 미각, 소비 습관, 그리고 심지어 자아 정체성까지 어떻게 깊이 있게 재구성하고 있는지를 성찰하게 만듭니다. 알고리즘이 추천하는 레시피 한 조각, 웰니스 음료 한 모금, 그리고 SNS에 올라오는 완벽한 가정식 사진 속에서 우리는 과연 무엇을 소비하고, 무엇을 추구하고 있는 것일까요?

# 현대 치료 문화의 어두운 이면: '트라우마 스크립트'의 함정

### 에피소드 요약

이 영상은 현대 치료 문화의 어두운 면을 파헤치며, ‘트라우마 스크립트(trauma script)’가 개인의 고통을 상품화하고 사회적·정치적 논의를 왜곡한다고 비판한다. 문화 이론가 캐서린 류(Katherine Leu) 교수는 19세기 후반 영국 철도 시스템에서 부유한 사업가들이 겪었던 ‘철도 척추(railway spine)’ 진단에서 현대 트라우마 개념이 시작되었다고 설명한다. 이는 신체적 상처 없이도 정신적 충격으로 인한 업무 불능을 주장하며 철도 회사를 고소하는 과정에서 심리 치료의 필요성이 부각된 사례다. 이처럼 트라우마는 원래 부유하고 유능한 남성의 피해를 보상하기 위한 의료 및 법률적 개념으로 출발했으며, 산업 혁명기 공장 노동자들의 죽음이나 만성적인 고통은 ‘그저 노동계급의 삶’으로 치부되며 트라우마로 인식되지 않았다는 점을 지적한다. 류 교수는 알렉산드리아 오카시오-코르테즈(Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez) 의원이 1월 6일 의사당 폭동 당시의 경험을 과거 성폭력 트라우마와 연결 지어 소셜 미디어에 공유한 사례를 들며, 개인의 트라우마 고백이 정치적 문제에 대한 진지한 토론을 가로막고 자기 최적화(self-optimization)의 형태로 변질될 수 있다고 비판한다.

류 교수는 이러한 트라우마 스크립트가 계급 갈등을 은폐하고 자본주의 체제의 문제를 회피하는 기제로 작동한다고 주장한다. 개인의 고통을 공론화하는 것이 금기를 깨고 대화의 장을 넓히는 긍정적인 측면도 있지만, 궁극적으로는 사회의 불평등과 경제적 어려움 같은 거시적인 문제에서 시선을 돌리게 만든다는 것이다. 시청자들은 자신의 고통을 공개적으로 드러내고 ‘생존자(survivor)’ 정체성을 통해 특별한 위치를 부여받으려는 경향을 경계하고, 개인적인 치유를 넘어 사회 구조적인 문제에 대한 비판적 사고와 집단적 행동의 필요성을 인식해야 한다. 트라우마를 개인적인 문제로만 국한하는 대신, 더 나은 사회를 만들기 위한 교육, 합리적인 논쟁, 그리고 강력한 언론의 역할에 주목해야 할 것이다.

오늘날 우리는 전례 없는 '트라우마 시대'를 살고 있습니다. 개인의 고통과 상처를 이야기하고 치유하는 것이 미덕처럼 여겨지며, 공적 담론의 장까지 개인적인 '트라우마 서사'로 가득 차 있습니다. 하지만 이러한 현상이 과연 건강한 것일까요? 팟캐스트 '더 그레이 에어리어(The Gray Area)'에 출연한 문화 이론가 캐서린 루(Katherine Leu) 어바인 캘리포니아 대학교 영화 및 미디어학과 교수는 자신의 신간 『트라우마화된: 공적 고통의 새로운 정치(Traumatized: The New Politics of Public Suffering)』를 통해 현대 사회의 '트라우마 스크립트(trauma script)'가 가진 어두운 이면을 날카롭게 비판합니다. 막시스트(Marxist) 관점에서 정치적 행동이 개인적인 '수행(performance)'에 의해 가려지고 있다고 주장하는 루 교수는, 이른바 '트라우마 문화'가 개인의 진정한 치유는 물론, 민주주의와 지적 토론에까지 해를 끼치고 있다고 경고합니다.

## '트라우마 스크립트'란 무엇인가?

[[SUM]] 현대 사회에서 개인의 고통을 정형화된 서사로 만들어 공론화하고 자격 증명처럼 활용하는 상품화된 트라우마 서사는 개인을 소외시키고 민주적 토론을 방해한다.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:95]]

루 교수는 현대 사회에서 개인의 트라우마가 '상품화된 서사(commodified trauma script)'로 변질되어 무분별하게 공유되고 있다고 지적합니다. 이는 특히 젊은 세대에게 두드러지는데, 하버드 크림슨(Harvard Crimson) 학생들이 개인 에세이를 쓸 때 트라우마 스토리가 필수적인 요소로 여겨졌던 사례는 이를 방증합니다. 자신의 트라우마를 틀에 박힌 방식으로 이야기하고, 이를 공론화하며 '자격 증명(credential)'처럼 활용하는 행위는 결국 자기 자신을 객관화(objectify)하고 자신의 경험으로부터 소외(alienate)시키는 결과를 낳는다는 것입니다. 루 교수는 이러한 '상품화된 트라우마 서사'가 개인에게도, 민주주의에도, 그리고 건전한 토론과 지적 생활에도 결코 좋지 않다고 단언합니다.

## 트라우마 개념의 의외의 기원

[[SUM]] 트라우마는 19세기 영국 철도 사고 후 신체 부상 없는 부유한 사업가들의 손해 배상 소송을 돕기 위한 '철도 척추' 진단과 의료적 보상 과정에서 우발적으로 발명되었다.

[[AT:JysbLAm7uL4:126]]

우리가 오늘날 이해하는 '트라우마' 개념의 현대적 기원은 의외의 곳에서 찾아볼 수 있습니다. 19세기 후반, 영국에서는 새로운 기술이었던 철도 시스템이 도입되면서 속도에 대한 두려움과 철도 사고가 빈번하게 발생했습니다. 런던과 북부를 오가던 부유한 사업가들은 신체적 부상 없이도 사고 후 극심한 공포와 충격으로 인해 정상적인 업무를 수행할 수 없게 되는 경우가 많았습니다.

당시 의사들은 이를 '철도 척추(railway spine)'라는 진단명으로 설명했습니다. 사고 시 척추의 흔들림이 뇌진탕을 일으켜 업무 불능 상태로 만들었다는 것입니다. 이는 곧 소송 문제로 번졌고, 사업가들은 철도 회사에 손해 배상을 청구했습니다. 비록 신체적 상처는 없었지만, 마치 오늘날의 외상 후 스트레스 장애(PTSD)와 유사한 '회상(flashbacks)' 증상에 시달렸던 이들은 의학적 치료와 보상을 요구했습니다. 루 교수는 이러한 과정이 "부유하고 유능한 전문직 남성"의 신체에 가해진 "피해와 충격"이라는 매우 구체적인 맥락에서 "우발적인 심리 치료의 발명(inadvertent invention of psychotherapy)"으로 이어졌다고 설명합니다.

반면, 철도 발명 이전의 인류 문명에서 고통, 충격, 운명의 일격은 사회적, 종교적 의미를 가졌습니다. 신의 벌로 여겨지거나, 단순히 인내해야 할 것으로 받아들여졌습니다. 내면의 삶은 수리될 수 있는 것이 아니라 감내해야 할 것이었고, 인내와 끈기 자체가 높은 가치를 지녔습니다. '회복'이나 '치료'라는 개념은 트라우마가 세속화되고 의학 및 법률 기관을 통해 의미를 부여받으면서 비로소 등장한 것입니다.

루 교수는 고대 서사시 '오디세이(Odyssey)'를 현대적으로 각색한 크리스토퍼 놀란(Christopher Nolan)의 시도를 비판적으로 언급합니다. 오디세우스가 겪는 운명의 일격은 본래 신들의 분노로 설명되지만, 놀란은 이를 오디세우스의 PTSD, 즉 트로이 전쟁에서의 죄책감과 트라우마로 인한 고통으로 재해석합니다. 이는 수천 년 전부터 존재했던 강력한 여신(칼립소)의 이미지를 단순한 '치료사'로 격하시키는 것이며, 고통의 의미가 개인의 심리적 문제로 환원되는 현대적 경향을 보여준다는 것입니다.

루 교수는 진정한 '진정성(authenticity)'과 '공감(real empathy)'은 우리와 가까운 사람들을 위해, 그리고 자기 인식을 향한 개인적인 여정으로서 보존되어야 한다고 믿습니다. 끊임없이 무심하게 공유되는 '상품화된 트라우마 서사'는 개인에게도, 민주주의에도, 토론과 지적 생활에도 좋지 않습니다.

1.  **더욱 엄밀한 역사적 이해:** 우리가 오늘날의 상황에 어떻게 도달했는지에 대한 깊이 있는 역사적 이해가 필요합니다.
2.  **현실 도피 욕구에 대한 자기 성찰:** 우리 자신의 현실 도피 욕구에 대해 솔직하게 직면하고 성찰해야 합니다. 인스타그램의 강아지 구조 영상에 중독되는 것처럼, 세상의 끔찍한 현실 앞에서 무력감을 느끼는 우리 자신의 모습을 인정해야 합니다.
