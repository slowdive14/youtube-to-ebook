# The Shifting Sands of Stardom: When Everyone's Famous, Is Anyone?

### Episode summary

This "It's Been a Minute" episode explores how "celebrity is a state of mind" in an era where anyone can achieve fame. Co-hosts Bobby Finger and Lindsay Weber, authors of *I Want to Be Famous*, argue that the traditional A-list "them" is being eclipsed by "who's" – individuals like Olivia Jade (daughter of Lori Loughlin), who gain immense success and wealth through vlogging despite lacking conventional celebrity credentials. The hosts highlight that lower-level fame offers greater freedom, citing Meghan Trainor's pet food jingle as an example of a "who" leveraging opportunities a "them" might avoid.

The practical takeaway is that the value of "true fame" has shifted from money to creative control, as "lowercase f" famous individuals can now be richer than "capital F" stars. The discussion also critiques Pete Davidson's comments on influencers, noting his own fame stems from his personal life and "who-them" relationships, like dating Ariana Grande, rather than solely his work. Reality TV is identified as a key catalyst, transforming fame by allowing ordinary people to become household names and monetize their lives without traditional career paths.

In an era where a single viral video can launch a career and a carefully curated Instagram feed can build an empire, the very definition of "celebrity" has undergone a radical transformation. No longer solely the domain of Hollywood elites or chart-topping musicians, fame has become a fractured, fluid concept – a state of mind, as some might argue. This seismic shift is at the heart of the insightful discussions led by Bobby Finger and Lindsay Weber, co-hosts of the popular podcast *Who Weekly* and authors of the new book *When Everybody and Nobody Is a Celebrity*.

On NPR's *It's Been a Minute*, Finger and Weber delved into the evolving landscape of stardom, dissecting how traditional fame ("capital F Famous") now coexists with a burgeoning class of "lowercase f famous" individuals. Their conversation explored the changing value of recognition, the unexpected ways money is made, the indelible mark of reality television, and the fierce loyalty of modern stan culture. The ultimate question they pose: If anyone can be famous, what is the true utility of fame anymore?

## The "Who" and the "Them": A New Binary of Stardom

[[SUM]] The celebrity hierarchy has shifted from A-D lists to a binary of "Thems" (universally recognizable) and "Whos" (niche or vaguely familiar), with examples like Olivia Jade and Oscar-winner Mikey Madison illustrating this distinction.

[[AT:Og7U9ZIpuE4:371]]

Gone are the days of a clear A, B, C, or D-list hierarchy. According to Finger and Weber, the contemporary celebrity ecosystem operates on a simpler, yet more complex, binary: "Thems" and "Whos."

"It used to be like if you go to a checkout counter at a grocery store and you see all the magazines, you see all the tabloids, you would see all these faces and either you would know who they were, those are them, or you would say, who the hell is this person? And that would be a who," Bobby Finger explains. This distinction, born from the fading print media landscape, now applies to the digital realm. A "Them" is universally recognizable, a household name. A "Who" is someone whose fame is niche, context-dependent, or simply vague – a face you've seen, a name you've heard, but can't quite place.

The hosts illustrate this with compelling examples. Lindsay Weber's current favorite "Who" is Olivia Jade, daughter of Lori Loughlin, famous for her role in the college admissions scandal. Despite dropping out of college, Olivia Jade is "more successful than ever," a vlogger with a beauty line and a high-profile dating history. Her fame is tied to a specific narrative and digital presence, rather than traditional achievements.

Bobby Finger, on the other hand, points to Mikey Madison, an Oscar winner for *Anora*. Despite her critical acclaim and upcoming projects, Madison remains "unknowable." Finger attributes this to her intentional pushback against the celebrity machine, giving little of herself in interviews – a "Jeremy Strongian" quality. However, he notes, "it's different for men than it is for women," as women are often pressured more intensely to reveal their personal lives. This deliberate mystery, while alluring for some, can also make a female star seem "strange" in today's oversharing culture.

## The Shifting Economics of Fame: Freedom in Lowercase 'f'

[[SUM]] Fame and fortune are decoupling, allowing "lowercase f famous" individuals to be richer than "capital F famous" ones; they gain freedom to accept diverse opportunities without image concerns, a shift from past norms where such endorsements were hidden.

[[AT:Og7U9ZIpuE4:434]]

Perhaps the most surprising shift in the modern celebrity landscape is the decoupling of fame and fortune. "Sometimes these lowercase F famous people can be richer than the capital F famous people," notes the host of *It's Been a Minute*.

The traditional goal of fame was clear: "The goal was money, right? The goal was to be the highest paid star in Hollywood, the highest like the the top of the Billboard charts," Finger elaborates. "But you don't necessarily need the fame to make the money anymore."

This new reality offers a unique kind of freedom to the "Whos." Less famous individuals are not bound by the strict image management that governs A-listers. They can accept more diverse offers – from social media sponsorships to commercial jingles – without fear of being perceived as "trashy," "less classy," or "less rare." Megan Trainor singing a jingle for pet food, for example, might have raised eyebrows in a previous era, but now, as Lindsay Weber points out, "that's probably a great check that she just got," and the audience quickly forgets the product.

This phenomenon is not entirely new; George Clooney and Julia Roberts famously did ads for products in Japan that they would never endorse in the U.S. But with the internet, these "secrets" are out, and rather than being embarrassing, they've become "delightful," even celebrated as a form of capitalistic hustle. "Now it's not really stooping low anymore. It's just it's the level playing field. It's what everyone's doing," Finger observes.

While true A-listers still command creative control and choice in their projects, the path to financial stability has broadened considerably for those operating on a slightly lower tier of fame. Brands now recognize that a "Who" with a highly engaged, specific audience can be more valuable than a "Them" with broad but less targeted reach.

## Reality TV: The Great Democratizer of Stardom

[[SUM]] Reality TV transformed fame by allowing "normal people" to bypass traditional gatekeepers and monetize their public presence, with participants now explicitly aiming for stardom, making reality stardom one of the few fields gaining security.

[[AT:Og7U9ZIpuE4:1241]]

"Reality TV didn't just change our viewing habits but the nature of fame itself," Bobby Finger asserts. It served as a massive training-wheels-off moment, allowing "normal people" to bypass traditional industry gatekeepers and catapult directly into public consciousness.

In the early days of shows like *The Real World*, participants would typically return to their regular lives after their season aired. But as reality television evolved, producers and participants alike realized the immense potential for monetization and career longevity. Elizabeth Hasselbeck, who parlayed her *Survivor* stint into a role on *The View*, became an early example of this shift.

Today, the intention behind appearing on a reality show is starkly different. "When people go on *Love Island* or *Love is Blind* or any of these reality shows, *The Bachelor*, they go in saying, 'This is a means to an end.' And then I get to be famous," Weber explains. This has created a paradoxical situation where, in an increasingly precarious job market, "reality stardom is one of the few fields that seems to be getting more secure." Shows like *The Challenge* have created multi-decade careers for their participants.

The appeal lies in its bankability. While traditional talent, like a beautiful singing voice, struggles to monetize in an age of streaming, "influencing" proves far more lucrative. "Music is not monetizable like so many other things," Weber argues. "It is more lucrative to become like a vlogger or, you know, some other like skill that is in an industry where people are used to paying for that thing." Platforms like OnlyFans, despite their reputation, exemplify direct patronage, where fans directly support creators, often more profitably than traditional entertainment avenues. Follower counts on TikTok and Instagram, while not direct money, act as a form of patronage, directly influencing a person's market value and casting opportunities.

## Stan Culture: Keepers of the Flame and Controllers of Discourse

[[SUM]] Stan armies, like Taylor Swift's "Swifties," build community and identity, actively controlling online discourse through decoding "Easter eggs" and preserving their idol's legacy, with monetization strategies adapting as stars and fans age.

[[AT:Og7U9ZIpuE4:1804]]

The internet, while fracturing fame, also created powerful new communities: stan armies. Taylor Swift, with her legions of "Swifties," is the ultimate emblem of this phenomenon. Stan culture is about "community building... identity," where choosing to be a stan can define who you are.

These passionate fan bases do more than just build community; they actively "control the online discourse." Swift's strategic "Easter eggs" are a prime example: she knows that a small percentage of her fans will obsessively decode these clues, generating endless conversation and dominating the online space. Ultimately, stans become "the keepers of the flame," preserving their idol's legacy.

As these stars – and their fans – age, the monetization strategies adapt. Beyoncé's highly-priced 20th-anniversary *B'Day* vinyl box set, for example, is a clear appeal to an aging, more affluent fanbase. "A 40-year-old can afford a $900 box," Finger quips.

The looming question, however, is whether younger generations will continue this intense, often "toxic" stan behavior. While some hope for a shift, Lindsay Weber believes, "from what I've seen so far, I don't think so. I don't think that's the case." The behavior, it seems, is here to stay, at least within the pop music sphere.

## Rita Ora: The Future of Celebrity

[[SUM]] Rita Ora, a multi-hyphenate "working star" who embraces every opportunity, embodies the future of celebrity; her once-confusing, multifaceted career is now becoming the norm in the gig economy of fame, where constant visibility and monetization are key.

[[AT:Og7U9ZIpuE4:2084]]

To encapsulate the future of fame, Finger and Weber turn to their long-standing "Who Weekly" muse: Rita Ora. Ora, a British singer, actress, model, and host, has been a consistent presence in the spotlight for a decade, yet remains somewhat "nebulous" in her primary identity.

"She does everything. So it's sometimes unclear to people what it is the thing that she does," Finger explains. This multi-hyphenate approach, taking every opportunity that comes her way – hosting, modeling, acting, singing, entrepreneurship, sponsorships – defines the "gig economy" of fame. She is a "working star," always "booked and busy."

Despite the ambiguity of her core talent, Ora is "clicky as hell" for tabloids, consistently generating content and maintaining relevance. "There has never been a week where there's nothing to say about what she is up to," Weber states.

Rita Ora, in essence, was ahead of the curve. Ten years ago, her multifaceted career might have seemed confusing, especially to American audiences accustomed to more defined celebrity roles. But now, with the rise of influencer culture, the decline of shame around "selling out," and the prevalence of celebrities taking every available job, "this is becoming the norm. And so Rita is a little bit less out of place than she was 10 years ago because kind of everyone's doing what she's doing. She just kind of started it."

In this new landscape, celebrity is no longer a fixed destination but a dynamic, ongoing performance. It's a strategic state of mind, a constant negotiation between visibility, monetization, and authenticity, where the lines between "Who" and "Them" blur, and everyone is trying to figure out how to stay booked and busy.

리타 오라는 이러한 '유명세의 긱 경제(gig economy)'를 선도했습니다. 10년 전에는 미국 대중에게 라디오에 나오면서 쇼를 진행하고 잡지 표지에 등장하지만 음악 홍보를 하지 않는 그녀의 존재가 혼란스러웠을 것입니다. 그러나 인플루언서 문화의 부상, 수치심의 상실, 그리고 유명인들의 '긱 경제'는 이제 일반적인 현상이 되었습니다. 리타 오라는 더 이상 10년 전처럼 이질적인 존재가 아닙니다. 모두가 그녀가 하는 일을 하고 있기 때문입니다.

[[SUM]] 바비는 스토브를 켜놓고 왔다는 불안감을, 린지는 JFK가 살해되지 않았다는 믿음을 '제정신이 아닌' 믿음으로 꼽았다.

대화의 말미에 진행자는 바비와 린지에게 "가장 진정으로 '제정신이 아닌(unhinged)' 믿음이 무엇이냐"고 묻습니다. 바비는 자신의 불안감이 "내가 스토브를 켜놓고 왔어"라는 생각으로 나타난다며, 마지막으로 스토브를 켠 것이 언제든 상관없이 항상 그렇게 믿는다고 답해 웃음을 자아냅니다. 린지는 더 나아가 "JFK가 살해되지 않았다"고 믿는다며, 그가 한동안 살아있었고 잘 지냈다고 말해 모두를 놀라게 합니다.

이처럼 유명세의 본질을 파고드는 이들의 대화는 현대 사회에서 유명세가 얼마나 다면적이고 유동적인 개념이 되었는지 보여줍니다. 전통적인 A급 스타의 시대는 저물고, 누구나 자신만의 방식으로 유명세를 만들고 유지할 수 있는 '유명세의 긱 경제' 시대가 도래했습니다. 유명세는 이제 특정 직업이나 재능에 국한되지 않고, '마음먹기에 달린' 하나의 상태가 되고 있습니다.
