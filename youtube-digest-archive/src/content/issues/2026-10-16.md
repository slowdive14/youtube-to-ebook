# Beyond the Noise: Discovering Your True Self in the Silence

### Episode summary

Andrew Holecek argues that our addiction to distraction, fueled by a "deficient sense of emptiness," prevents us from truly knowing ourselves. He champions dark retreats—complete sensory deprivation—as a "cold plunge" into the unconscious, forcing us to confront "undigested, unprocessed psychic material" and the "primordial contraction" of self. Initially, 90% of people experience fight, flight, freeze, or fawn responses, but eventually, the mind "falls into itself," revealing a radical expansion of perception.

This intense experience, where "empty of self means full of others," ultimately dilates not just our eyes, but our minds and hearts. The practical takeaway is to cultivate this openness in daily life, transforming the revelatory state of non-grasping into a stable trait, rather than letting our perception boxes contract back into defensive mechanisms against the "sensory assault of our conventional world."

In an age where our lives are perpetually illuminated by screens and our minds are constantly buzzing with notifications, the idea of doing nothing feels not just counterintuitive, but almost terrifying. We are, as spiritual teacher Andrew Holecek observes, "light junkies," addicted to the constant stimulation and distraction that defines modern existence. This isn't just a casual habit; it's a profound cultural phenomenon rooted in a deep-seated unease within ourselves.

Just pause for a moment and consider your own impulses. How many times a day do you instinctively reach for your phone, your tablet, or check your email? Look beneath that impulsive action, Holecek urges, and you'll likely find a "contraction"—a subtle tightening, a "deficient sense of emptiness," a nagging feeling that "something's missing." This void, he argues, is the very birthplace of consumerism, driving an insatiable "I want this, I want that" mentality that pulls us further and further away from our authentic selves. We've become so entangled in what we do that our identity is almost entirely defined by our actions.

### The Radical Invitation to Stop Doing

[[SUM]] Our identity is defined by what we do, but our true self is revealed when we stop doing, which is a radical act that threatens the familiar structure of the self.

But what if the key to unlocking who we truly are lies not in more doing, but in the radical act of stopping? Holecek presents a transformative truth: "Who you think you are is defined by what you do. Who you really are is revealed when you finally stop doing." This isn't just about taking a break; it's about deliberately stepping into an environment where the very structure of the self feels threatened, where the familiar anchors of identity are stripped away.

Enter the concept of a dark retreat. Imagine, if you will, a "cold plunge" for the senses – a journey into a complete sensory deprivation chamber. No light, no sound (or minimal sound), no external stimuli to cling to. The initial shock, Holecek explains, comes not from the darkness itself, but "from the shock of doing nothing."

### Confronting the Void: The Initial Resistance

[[SUM]] Entering a sensory deprivation chamber, like a dark retreat, initially causes intense boredom and a "fight, flight, freeze, or fawn" response, but with time, the mind relaxes and begins to fall into itself.

At first, this experience can be "intensely boring," a truly painful ordeal for minds accustomed to constant input. The internal monologue screams: "I can't do this. I can't distract. I can't move in the normal ways." It's a primal confrontation with the self, and for about 90% of people entering such an environment, the immediate reaction is one of contraction – a "fight, flight, freeze, or fawn" response. The mind, desperate for its usual coping mechanisms, rebels against the enforced stillness.

Yet, as with any immersion, the more time you spend in this profound darkness, the more familiar it becomes. Slowly, gradually, a shift occurs. You begin to relax, to slow down, to surrender to the unfamiliar void. It’s then that something remarkable happens: "the mind starts to fall into itself."

### Journey into the Unconscious: Spiders, Snakes, and Self-Discovery

[[SUM]] In the absence of external stimuli, the mind becomes reality, revealing "undigested, unprocessed, unmetabolized psychic material" from the unconscious, allowing us to dismantle limited self-definitions.

[[AT:HdJxqW0-uZA:131]]

In the absence of external reality, the mind becomes the sole reality. This is where the true journey begins, a descent into the "dimension of the unconscious mind where all the spiders and snakes hang out." This isn't a metaphor for fear, but for the vast reservoir of "undigested, unprocessed, unmetabolized psychic material" that lurks beneath the surface of our conscious awareness. Reflections from childhood experiences, unresolved emotions, forgotten traumas – they all begin to surface, not to overwhelm, but "to be in fact digested and metabolized in the dark."

This is a painful but exquisitely insightful process. You begin to see, with startling clarity, the constructs you've built around yourself, realizing, "I am not this, I'm not that." It's like "opening the walls, expanding the walls of your perception box," dismantling the limited definitions you've held about who you are.

### Unveiling the Primordial Contraction

[[SUM]] The journey delves deeper to the "primordial contraction," the core sense of self, which, when opened, forces a radical introduction to who you truly are by dissolving self-structure identity.

[[AT:HdJxqW0-uZA:196]]

But the journey goes even deeper. Eventually, you drop below these surface layers, all the way down to what Holecek calls the "primordial contraction." This isn't just an emotion or a memory; it "feels like me," like the very sense of self, the fundamental "I" that we take as a given. It's the core structure of our perceived individuality.

And then comes the ultimate invitation: to open it. "That's where the gold takes place," Holecek reveals. You are forced into a "radical introduction to who you really are," stripped of all the layers of identity you've accumulated. This environment brings about a "dissolution of self structure identity." You effectively "disappear to become nothing," dropping "below the surface structures of your storyline."

### Nothing Means Everything: The Ultimate Expansion

[[SUM]] In this profound emptiness, the self becomes full of others, everything dilates, and perception boxes expand, revealing boundless awareness and millions of points of light representing the unconscious mind.

In this profound emptiness, a paradox emerges: "nothing really means everything." When the self, as we conventionally understand it, is emptied, it becomes "full of others." This isn't a loss but an ultimate expansion. "Everything dilates," Holecek says, not just your eyes adjusting to the dark, but your mind, your heart, your body. "Everything opens." This is the ultimate freedom, the "ultimate expansion of all your perception boxes." With the external light gone, you look up and "see nothing but blue sky," a metaphor for the boundless awareness that emerges. Yet, in the dark, this allows for the revelation of "literally millions of stars, points of light that represent the unconscious mind."

### The Return: Holding the Openness

[[SUM]] Upon returning, the challenge is to hold this expanded openness and integrate it into daily life, transforming the state into a stable trait of less grasping and deeper connection to the true self.

[[AT:HdJxqW0-uZA:290]]

As you slowly emerge from this profound experience, returning to the conventional world, the "walls of your perception box contract" once more. They serve as necessary defense mechanisms against the overwhelming sensory assault of our daily lives. But the experience leaves an indelible mark. You realize, with profound clarity, "Oh my gosh, I don't need to grasp, I don't need to contract. I can stay open."

This realization is the true revelation, the invitation to a new way of being. The challenge then becomes: "Can I hold this level of openness? My sense faculties are open, my mind is open, my heart's open." How do you digest, metabolize, incorporate, and make this expanded state of awareness stable in the hurly-burly of everyday life? "How do I transform this state into a trait?"

This, Andrew Holecek concludes, is the journey. It's the ongoing work of integrating the profound insights gained in the silence and darkness into a life lived with greater openness, less grasping, and a deeper connection to the true self that lies beyond the constant hum of distraction. It's a testament to the transformative power of daring to do nothing, and in doing so, discovering everything.

# Beyond the Apocalypse: Hernan Diaz Explores Technology, Selfhood, and Hope in 'Ply'

### Episode summary

Hernan Diaz's novel, *Ply*, explores a future where institutions have crumbled, revealing a deeply personal journey about selfhood, identity, and intimacy. Initially conceived as a book about systems and community, Diaz discovered in the writing process that it ultimately asks, "Who am I? And how can I hope to ever relate to you?" The story follows a young, orphaned "tinker" in a future New York-like city, adept at siphoning electricity for avant-garde punk bands, which embroils her in a conspiracy leading to a machine capable of altering spacetime and reality.

Diaz, who deliberately avoids a singular authorial voice, set *Ply* in the future to explore technology's role in understanding America and to allow for stylistic reinvention. He posits that technology, from language to modern inventions, is how humans apprehend the world. The novel suggests that the collapse of the modern state, while painful, ushers in hope: new forms of community, family, and a renewed relationship with nature, exemplified by areas like Chernobyl where nature quickly reclaims land once humanity, "the plague that is us," recedes.

Hernan Diaz, the Pulitzer Prize-winning author of the acclaimed novel *Trust*, is no stranger to delving into the intricate mechanisms of power and greed. Yet, with his latest work, *Ply*, Diaz takes a radical departure, propelling readers into a future dramatically reshaped by climate change and institutional collapse. While *Trust* meticulously unraveled the financial machinations of the 19th and 20th centuries, *Ply* ventures into a world where technology and nature have blurred, and the very definition of self is under profound re-evaluation.

In a recent interview, Diaz revealed that the novel's journey surprised even him, evolving from an exploration of systems and community into a deep dive into the essence of individual identity. "How we think about ourselves, how we see ourselves, when that stuff changes, the ripple effects can be fairly profound," he muses, hinting at the powerful undercurrents that drive *Ply*.

## A Glimpse into a Future Unmade and Remade

[[SUM]] In a future New York where the state has collapsed and climate change has rewilded the landscape, an orphaned "tinker" becomes a "pincher" of electricity, leading her into a conspiracy involving a spacetime-altering machine, but ultimately revealing a story about selfhood, identity, and intimacy.

[[AT:RleRCXFM4ZE:62]]

*Ply* unfolds in a future vaguely shaped after New York, a landscape where the state as an institution has vanished. Climate change has drastically altered the world, leaving behind a series of scattered city-states and rewilded territories. Diaz paints a vivid picture of this transformed environment: "The small cluster of islands to the north and the large ones due south appear to hover mute and blind above the horizon. It was hard to imagine the violence that kept each one of the placid stars shining against the cleanest black possible. Except for a few distant fires, there was nothing on the main coastline."

At the heart of this fractured world is a young, orphaned protagonist. She is a "tinker," a resourceful individual adept at fiddling with and repurposing old objects and technology in a world where new industrial production is rare. Her craftiness eventually leads her to become a "pincher," someone who siphons electricity directly from the grid. This introduces one of the novel's central themes: power, explored in both its political and literal, electrical senses. "This is a novel about power in at least two different senses," Diaz explains. "The crisis of political power, of course, the institution of the state is largely gone... but also electric power and physical forces in a very literal way."

The protagonist's illicit activities lead her into a bizarre conspiracy, culminating in the invention of a massive machine capable of altering spacetime and reality itself. Yet, for all its futuristic complications and intricate plot turns, Diaz found the true core of *Ply* during the writing process. "This is a novel about selfhood, about identity, and about intimacy. Who am I? And how can I hope to ever relate to you? And what is this space between us? This physical space between us that supposedly separates us but actually might conjoin us," he reflects. Beyond the grand sci-fi elements, *Ply* emerges as a deeply human story of frailty, resilience, trust, betrayal, found family, and ultimately, love.

## Why the Future? Diaz's Unconventional Approach to Storytelling

[[SUM]] Diaz chose a futuristic setting to explore technology's role in the U.S. and to fulfill his desire as a writer to disappear within his work by reinventing his thematic and stylistic approach with each new book.

[[AT:RleRCXFM4ZE:285]]

Diaz's decision to leap from the historical realism of *Trust* to the speculative future of *Ply* was deliberate, driven by a two-fold ambition. Firstly, he sought to engage with technology as a fundamental lens through which to understand the United States. "I don't think you can understand the United States, at least since the industrial revolution, without considering technology," he asserts, noting that the "natural habitat of technology seems to be the future."

Secondly, and perhaps more tellingly, Diaz reveals a unique philosophical stance on his own authorial presence. "My greatest desire as a writer is to never have a voice," he confides. "I don't want to have a voice of my own. I want to disappear in what I write. When I find myself on the page, I view that as an enormous failure." This commitment to authorial self-effacement drives him to reinvent his thematic, stylistic, and even grammatical approach with each new book, ensuring that every narrative offers a fresh and distinct experience.

## Nature, Technology, and the Human Condition

[[SUM]] The novel depicts a rewilded world where nature rapidly reclaims territory, and technology is broadly defined as how humans understand and relay impressions of the world, with language being the ultimate machine; this highlights humanity's persistent, yet often hubristic, drive for knowledge, integral to the novel's scientific passages.

[[AT:RleRCXFM4ZE:350]]

The world of *Ply* is one where nature has spectacularly reclaimed its territory. Trees grow through derelict buildings, and entire areas are swallowed by weeds and swamps. This "rewilding" isn't merely a backdrop; it's a profound statement on humanity's impact. Diaz cites examples like Chernobyl and the Korean DMZ, areas untrodden by humans, where "it's crazy how fast nature bounces back once the plague that is us goes away."

But where does technology fit into this rewilded future? Diaz challenges conventional notions, arguing that technology is far more than just "male techno chachkis." For him, technology is "the way in which humans apprehend, understand and relay their impressions of the world." In this expansive definition, language itself stands as the "greatest, most important, impressive machine that we've ever built," a precursor to all subsequent inventions.

This perspective leads to a deeper exploration of human nature. Even as institutions crumble, an "unquenchable thirst, this appetite for knowledge" persists, which Diaz considers "our most beautiful trait." However, it's also linked to hubris and arrogance, forming a "doomed, beautifully, gorgeously doomed struggle" that defines the history of human thought, science, philosophy, and art. The novel, surprisingly, contains substantial scientific passages, which Diaz defends by drawing parallels to literary giants: "Try to read *Frankenstein* without the passages on natural philosophy. Try to read *Moby Dick* without the passages on cetology. Nothing is left of the novel." These passages, he insists, are integral, not just intellectually stimulating but also brimming with heart and emotion.

## The Collapsed State and Evolving Selfhood

[[SUM]] The title *Ply* signifies the end of modernity and the state's collapse, which, despite losses, fosters hope for new social structures, non-traditional families, and a flourishing of arts; this context intensifies the novel's central question of "What is self?", explored through technology and the intertwined lives of three women, while certain enclaves like the Great Lakes region maintain the old order.

[[AT:RleRCXFM4ZE:380]]

The title *Ply* itself alludes to a "historical fold," marking the painful end of modernity. With the collapse of the state, humanity loses much that is "extremely hurtful to lose"—education, health, the rule of law. Yet, amidst this pain, Diaz finds "enormous hope." The absence of the modern state, which was not always present, allows for the emergence of new forms of sociality, communal organizations, non-patriarchal family structures, novel economies, and a welcome lack of surveillance, fostering a flourishing of the arts and a new way of coexisting with nature.

The central question *Ply* grapples with, "What is self?", becomes even more pressing in this transformed society. Diaz believes that shifts in our understanding of selfhood can trigger profound, centuries-long paradigmatic changes, beginning with the very definition of what it means to be human. In *Ply*, this plays out through the mediation of technology, which reshapes the notion of experience and leads to the emergence of new communities centered around art, music, and non-traditional family structures. At its core, the novel follows the intertwined trajectories of three women at different stages of life, exploring how they interact, interfere, and even superimpose as people, delving into the complexities of friendship and love.

Interestingly, while the United States as a union has dissolved in *Ply*, certain enclaves persist. The Great Lakes region, for instance, has become a haven for the well-to-do, where the "old order" based on private property, profit, and instrumental societal organization has managed to endure. It's a subtle but significant detail, suggesting that even in a future of radical change, some echoes of the past remain stubbornly resilient.

Hernan Diaz’s *Ply* is not merely a futuristic tale; it's a meticulously crafted thought experiment that uses the canvas of a collapsed world to dissect the enduring questions of human existence. By pushing the boundaries of setting and genre, Diaz once again proves his mastery in revealing the profound depths of human experience, even as he strives to disappear within the pages of his own creation.

## 결론

[[SUM]] 디아즈의 '플라이'는 파괴된 미래 속에서 '자아'를 탐색하며, 기술, 자연, 사회 변화 속에서 인간이 자신을 재정의하는 통찰을 제공한다.

허난 디아즈의 '플라이'는 단순히 미래를 배경으로 한 공상 과학 소설을 넘어선다. 파괴된 세계의 거대한 풍경 속에서, 작가는 인간 존재의 가장 근본적인 질문인 '자아'를 탐색한다. 기술, 자연, 사회 시스템의 변화 속에서 인간이 어떻게 자신을 재정의하고 새로운 관계를 맺어가는지에 대한 디아즈의 통찰은 독자들에게 깊은 울림을 선사한다. 그의 작품은 매번 새로운 스타일과 주제로 독자를 놀라게 하지만, 그 근원에는 언제나 인간 본성에 대한 예리한 시선과 섬세한 탐구가 자리하고 있다. '플라이'는 우리가 살고 있는 시대의 혼란 속에서 우리 자신과 우리의 관계를 되돌아보게 하는 중요한 질문을 던지는 작품이 될 것이다.
