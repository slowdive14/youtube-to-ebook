# Unleashing the Innovator: Why Microsoft's CTO Sees a Future Without Gatekeepers

### Episode summary

Microsoft CTO Kevin Scott champions permissionless innovation, arguing that the current technological landscape uniquely empowers individuals to build and deploy ideas without needing approval from gatekeepers. He highlights how today's accessible tools and platforms bypass traditional hurdles, enabling creators to directly reach beneficiaries with their innovations.

The practical takeaway is to embrace this era of reduced friction and act on your ideas, knowing that the path from concept to impact is more direct than ever. While not explicitly stated, the implicit nuance is that this freedom comes with a responsibility to innovate ethically and thoughtfully.

In an era defined by rapid technological advancement, the pace of innovation is accelerating at an unprecedented rate. Yet, for many aspiring creators and problem-solvers, the path from a brilliant idea to a tangible product or service has historically been fraught with hurdles. These hurdles often come in the form of gatekeepers – institutions, individuals, or processes that stand between an innovator and the people who might benefit from their creation. But what if those gates are starting to crumble? What if the very structure of innovation is shifting towards a future where permission is no longer a prerequisite?

This profound shift is precisely what excites leaders in the tech world, including Microsoft's Chief Technology Officer. The CTO articulates a vision where the most thrilling aspect of the current technological landscape is "the extent to which you can go innovate and build things without having to seek someone's permission." This isn't just about convenience; it represents a fundamental re-architecture of how ideas come to life and impact the world.

### The Tyranny of Permission

[[SUM]] Historically, innovators faced "complicated gatekeeping things" like VCs, corporations, and regulators, which stifled creativity and centralized power by controlling access to funding, tools, and markets.

For decades, the journey of an innovator often resembled an obstacle course. Whether an entrepreneur seeking funding, a developer needing access to proprietary tools, or a researcher navigating bureaucratic approvals, the process was often characterized by "complicated gatekeeping things that are sitting in between you who are the person who had the idea and like the people who might benefit from" it.

These gatekeepers, while sometimes serving necessary functions like quality control or resource allocation, frequently became bottlenecks. They could include:

*   **Venture Capitalists and Investors:** The traditional arbiters of which ideas get funded and which don't, often based on market trends, perceived risk, or even personal biases.
*   **Large Corporations:** Controlling access to critical infrastructure, APIs, or distribution channels, making it difficult for smaller players to compete or integrate.
*   **Regulatory Bodies:** Imposing complex compliance requirements that can be prohibitively expensive and time-consuming for startups.
*   **Platform Owners:** Dictating terms, fees, and approval processes for apps or services built on their ecosystems.

The consequence of such gatekeeping is often a stifling of creativity. Promising ideas might never see the light of day, or their development might be slowed to a crawl, missing critical windows of opportunity. It centralizes power and decision-making, limiting the diversity of innovations that emerge.

### The Rise of Permissionless Innovation

[[SUM]] Permissionless innovation, the antithesis of gatekeeping, is now possible due to open-source software, cloud computing, decentralized technologies like Web3, no-code platforms, and global connectivity, empowering individuals to build without explicit approval.

The concept of "permissionless innovation" is the antithesis of this gatekeeping model. It champions an environment where individuals and small teams can develop, deploy, and scale their creations without needing explicit approval from a centralized authority. This paradigm shift is not merely theoretical; it's being powered by several converging technological and cultural trends:

1.  **Open Source Movement:** The proliferation of open-source software, frameworks, and tools has democratized development. Anyone with an internet connection can access, modify, and build upon a vast library of code, free from licensing fees or corporate restrictions.
2.  **Cloud Computing and Infrastructure as Code:** Platforms like Azure, AWS, and Google Cloud provide instant access to scalable computing power, storage, and specialized services. Innovators no longer need to raise millions for data centers; they can provision infrastructure on demand, paying only for what they use. This dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for launching sophisticated applications.
3.  **Decentralized Technologies (Web3, Blockchain):** Emerging technologies like blockchain aim to build truly permissionless systems. Smart contracts, decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs), and cryptocurrencies allow for the creation of self-governing applications and economies where trust is embedded in code, not reliant on intermediaries.
4.  **No-Code/Low-Code Platforms:** Tools that enable individuals without extensive programming knowledge to build functional applications and websites are empowering a new generation of citizen developers, further broadening the base of potential innovators.
5.  **Global Connectivity and Collaboration:** The internet itself, coupled with collaborative tools, allows ideas to spread globally and teams to form across continents, bypassing traditional geographic or corporate boundaries.

### The Benefits: A New Era of Creativity and Impact

[[SUM]] This shift democratizes opportunity, accelerates development, fosters diverse solutions, and empowers individuals, leading to an unprecedented wave of creativity and problem-solving globally.

The implications of a permissionless future are profound. It promises to unlock an unprecedented wave of creativity and problem-solving:

*   **Democratization of Opportunity:** Anyone with an idea and the drive to build can now do so, regardless of their background, connections, or access to traditional capital. This levels the playing field, allowing brilliant minds from all corners of the globe to contribute.
*   **Accelerated Pace of Development:** Without the delays associated with seeking permission, innovations can iterate faster, respond more quickly to market needs, and reach beneficiaries in record time.
*   **Diverse Solutions:** When more people can innovate, a wider array of perspectives and experiences are brought to bear on problems, leading to more varied, inclusive, and sometimes unexpected solutions.
*   **Empowerment of the Individual:** Permissionless innovation shifts power from institutions to individuals and small, agile teams, fostering a culture of self-reliance and direct impact.

### Navigating the Future

[[SUM]] While exciting, this new frontier requires careful consideration of challenges like security and ethical use, as the goal is to ensure structures support innovation rather than hinder it, empowering anyone with an idea to make an impact.

While the excitement for permissionless innovation is palpable, it's also important to acknowledge that this new frontier comes with its own set of challenges. Questions around security, ethical use, quality assurance, and responsible governance in a decentralized world will need careful consideration. The goal isn't to dismantle all structures, but to ensure that structures serve innovation rather than hindering it.

Ultimately, the Microsoft CTO's enthusiasm highlights a significant turning point. We are moving towards a world where the power to create and make an impact is increasingly in the hands of anyone with an idea and the tools to bring it to life. This shift promises a future teeming with novel solutions, unexpected breakthroughs, and a truly global tapestry of innovation, woven by countless individuals who no longer need to ask for permission to build a better tomorrow.

# Beyond the Porta-Potty: Mastering the Art of Music Festival Etiquette

### Episode summary

NPR's Pop Culture Happy Hour panel tackles essential music festival etiquette to avoid being "that person," particularly as summer brings peak festival season. Host Aisha Harris and co-hosts Stephen Thompson and Isabella Gomez Sarmiento passionately argue against the notion that the front row is the best spot, citing instances of people "soiling themselves" to maintain a prime view, and Aisha's personal anecdote of a friend passing out from heat and dehydration while crammed at the front for Kendrick Lamar. They contend that the front row offers poor sound, obstructed views for shorter attendees, and potential hearing damage from massive speakers, advocating instead for spaces near the soundboard or further back for dancing and better overall enjoyment.

The practical takeaway is to prioritize the in-the-moment, communal experience over capturing content or aggressively pushing through crowds. The panel strongly advises against excessive phone use, noting poor signal and the "content farming" trend that detracts from live music, as evidenced by videos of Sabrina Carpenter's Coachella set where the crowd merely filmed Madonna's surprise appearance. For navigating crowds, they suggest moving in groups of no more than three people to avoid creating disruptive "long trains" and to embrace analog methods like a tall friend with a "funky hat" for finding your group. Ultimately, the advice is to "just be," enjoy the music, and engage with those around you rather than stressing over a perfect view or recording.

Summer. The air crackles with anticipation, the distant thrum of bass promises unforgettable nights, and thousands converge on sprawling fields and city parks for the annual pilgrimage: music festival season. For many, it's a sanctuary of sound, a vibrant tapestry of shared experiences, and a chance to see a dizzying array of beloved artists in one place. But beyond the euphoria, the sheer scale of these events often brings its own set of challenges, transforming what should be a blissful escape into a gauntlet of human interaction—and sometimes, outright frustration.

What separates a truly memorable festival experience from a miserable one often boils down to something surprisingly simple: etiquette. We’ve all encountered “that person”—the one who makes you question humanity's capacity for consideration. Recognizing this universal truth, the hosts of NPR's Pop Culture Happy Hour, Aisha Harris and Stephen Thompson, along with NPR Music producer Isabella Gomez Sarmiento, recently delved deep into the unspoken rules of festival behavior, offering a passionate plea for decorum and presence. Their insights highlight that while the music might be wild, your behavior doesn't have to be.

### The Front Row Fallacy: Why Closer Isn't Always Better

[[SUM]] The front row is overrated due to physical discomfort, obstructed views, and potential hearing damage; instead, aim for areas around the soundboard or open fields for better sound and space.

For many, the ultimate festival aspiration is to secure that coveted front-row spot, inches from their idols. But Stephen Thompson, a seasoned festival-goer, argues passionately that this prime real estate is, in fact, vastly overrated. "The front row is not actually the best place to see a concert," he asserts, challenging a long-held belief. His reasoning is multifaceted and compelling.

Firstly, there's the physical toll. Being "mashed up against barricades" is not just uncomfortable; it can be genuinely distressing and even dangerous in dense crowds. Thompson vividly recounts a recent, disturbing trend: individuals "soiling themselves in the front row of concerts because they don't want to lose their primo spot." While acknowledging that medical conditions exist, he clarifies he's referring to those making a conscious, albeit extreme, choice to prioritize proximity over basic bodily functions. This shocking revelation underscores the desperate, often misguided, lengths some will go to for a spot that, ironically, might not even be the best.

Aisha Harris echoes this sentiment with a personal anecdote, recalling a Kendrick Lamar concert where her friend passed out due to the heat and being "smooshed with everyone else" at the front. Such experiences transform the dream of a perfect view into a nightmare of discomfort and concern.

Beyond the physical discomfort, the front row often presents a compromised viewing and listening experience. Shorter individuals, like Aisha, find their view completely obstructed by taller bodies, rendering their proximity moot. Even for taller attendees like Stephen, there's the self-consciousness of blocking others' views. Then there's the sound: positioned directly under colossal speakers, your precious hearing is at risk, and the sound mix itself might be distorted. Stephen, now a hearing aid wearer due to years of unprotected concert attendance, warns, "the front row is often you're positioned right under these gigantic speakers. You're not necessarily getting the best sound... you're also just having your precious, precious hearing possibly permanently damaged."

So, if not the front row, then where? Thompson suggests areas around the soundboard often offer "unobstructed views" and a more balanced sound experience, without the suffocating crowds. Isabella Gomez Sarmiento adds that her most enjoyable festival moments have been "closer to the back or in an open field situation," where there's ample room to dance, better sightlines, and less stress. The takeaway? Prioritize comfort, space, and sound quality over a potentially miserable and dangerous squeeze at the very front.

### Unplug and Live: The Case Against Constant Filming

[[SUM]] Constant phone filming detracts from the communal, in-person experience, creates poor-quality content, and is often unnecessary given professional documentation, so put phones away and be present.

Perhaps no festival faux pas elicits more collective groans than the sea of glowing screens held aloft, perpetually recording. Isabella Gomez Sarmiento takes a firm stance: "I think there shouldn't be phones at music festivals or at least you shouldn't be predominantly on your phone at music festivals." Her argument is rooted in a desire to reclaim the communal, in-person experience that festivals are meant to foster.

In an era saturated with digital content, festivals offer a rare chance to truly disconnect. Yet, many attendees remain glued to their screens, not just for communication, but for "content farming." "Everyone wants the best video to post on TikTok," Isabella laments, arguing this pursuit is "so far removed from like the communal experience of live music." This obsession with documenting every moment often means missing the moment itself. She cites the disheartening sight of crowds at Sabrina Carpenter's Coachella set, where even a surprise appearance by Madonna was met with a forest of motionless phones. "It made me so sad and it made my skin crawl," she confesses, emphasizing the disservice to both the audience and the performing artist. Imagine performing to a crowd whose faces are obscured by glowing rectangles, their energy diverted from engagement to recording.

The practicalities also weigh against excessive phone use. Festival service is notoriously unreliable, making communication difficult anyway. Moreover, festivals are designed to be self-contained worlds, with clear signage for water, food, and medical aid, negating the constant need for digital navigation.

Stephen Thompson, whose work involves producing NPR's intimate Tiny Desk concerts, reveals that the issue is so pervasive that they've implemented a strict "phones away" policy after an initial photo op. This controlled environment underscores the importance of presence, a lesson that can—and should—be applied to the sprawling chaos of a festival.

While Aisha Harris concedes that a brief, personal video to remember friends might be acceptable, she questions the value of the vast majority of shaky, low-quality recordings. "Why do I need your really crappy, shaky cam video, where you can also probably see other people's phones in your video, because everyone is holding them up?" she asks. With professional videographers already documenting every performance, the personal amateur footage becomes redundant, a barrier to genuine enjoyment. The ultimate message: put the phone away, immerse yourself, and trust that the memory of the experience will be far richer than any pixelated video you might never watch again.

### The Three-Person Rule: Navigating Crowds with Grace

[[SUM]] When moving through crowds, limit your group to no more than three people at a time to avoid disrupting others, and use analog methods like plushies on sticks to find friends.

Beyond the personal choices of where to stand and whether to film, there's the collective challenge of navigating massive crowds. Aisha Harris identifies her "hill to die on" as the excruciating phenomenon of the "human train"—those seemingly endless single-file lines of people pushing through an already packed crowd.

"You see someone trying to pass through... and then all of a sudden another person is passing through and then another and then another and then finally it's like 10 people are passing through," she describes, articulating the frustration of being constantly bumped, inconvenienced, and having your view obstructed. This "long train that just goes on and on and on until the end of time is so disruptive."

Her solution is elegantly simple: "Pass with no more than three of you at a time." Three, she argues, is the perfect number—quick enough to pass without causing significant disruption, taking perhaps 15-20 seconds. Any more, and the inconvenience multiplies, creating a ripple effect of annoyance.

Stephen, who prefers to leave space around him for comfort and an "exit strategy," understands this pain intimately. He often finds himself a target for these human trains, as his considerate spacing is inevitably filled by surging groups. Isabella reinforces that most of these large groups are trying to reach the front, a futile endeavor given the already packed conditions. "You're probably not going to make it to the front. You're probably making the people who have been standing here like physically uncomfortable."

For those worried about finding friends in a large group, the hosts offer analog, creative solutions: one person carries a giant plushy animal on a stick, or a very tall friend wears a distinctive, funky hat. These visual beacons are far more effective than trying to text in a dead-signal zone. Ultimately, Isabella advises, if you're with a large group, "it ends up being like three people are in one part of the crowd, three people are going to go towards the back. Like you see each other after the set. Just like enjoy." The stress of trying to keep a large group together or push through a crowd actively detracts from the music.

Aisha, in a moment of playful exasperation, admits to becoming a "bridge patrol" if confronted with an endless human train, maneuvering herself to block further passage. It's a testament to the sheer frustration this common behavior causes.

### The Golden Rule of Festing: Just *Exist*

[[SUM]] The unifying philosophy is to "Just exist" by being present, aware, and considerate, prioritizing the experience over proximity, embracing presence, navigating with courtesy, and connecting with strangers.

Beneath these specific etiquette tips lies a unifying philosophy: "Just exist." This means being present, being aware, and being considerate of others sharing the space and the experience. "Festivals are supposed to be fun and good," Stephen reminds us, not "miserable camps in which we're all getting heatstroke and dehydrating and damaging our hearing."

The advice from the Pop Culture Happy Hour team boils down to a few core tenets that extend beyond the festival grounds and into "society" itself:

1.  **Prioritize the Experience, Not the Proximity:** The best spot isn't always the closest. Seek comfort, good sound, and space to move.
2.  **Embrace Presence, Ditch the Digital:** Put your phone away. Engage with the music, the artists, and the people around you. Create memories, not just content.
3.  **Navigate with Courtesy:** When moving through crowds, be mindful of those already settled. Limit your group size, and if you can't get through easily, find another path or accept your current location.
4.  **Connect with Strangers:** Look up from your phone, smile, talk to people. "Make friends with strangers," Isabella encourages. These spontaneous interactions are often as much a part of the festival magic as the music itself.
5.  **Be Self-Aware:** Consider your impact on others, whether you're tall and blocking a view, or pushing through a crowd.

Ultimately, the goal is to foster an environment where everyone can enjoy the music and the atmosphere without undue stress or discomfort. By adopting these simple yet profound guidelines, we can collectively transform the often chaotic music festival experience into what it’s truly meant to be: a joyful, communal celebration of sound and connection. So, as you pack your bags and prepare for the next big show, remember these unwritten rules. Your fellow festival-goers—and your future self—will thank you.

# The Assassin's Gambit: Luigi Mangione's Audacious Legal Play

### Episode summary

Luigi Mangione, the primary suspect in the assassination of United Healthcare CEO Brian Thompson, has employed a high-stakes legal strategy: pleading guilty to federal stalking charges that led to Thompson's death. This unexpected move, made in federal court, is a calculated attempt to invoke New York's broader double jeopardy protections and dismiss the looming state murder charges, which carry a 25-years-to-life sentence. His detailed allocution, admitting to planning the attack, using a 3D-printed gun, and traveling to New York with intent to kill, was central to establishing the federal conviction necessary for this defense.

The practical takeaway is that Mangione's team is betting on a single, potentially less severe federal sentence rather than facing two separate convictions. However, this is a risky gamble; if the double jeopardy argument fails, his detailed confession could be used against him in the state trial, and prosecutors have already publicly condemned his actions, stating "there can be no celebrity in assassination."

In the two years since a masked assailant gunned down healthcare executive Brian Thompson on a Manhattan sidewalk, the national spotlight has fixated on one primary suspect: Luigi Mangione. The December 2024 assassination, a brazen act that "riveted the nation," seemed to leave little doubt about Mangione's culpability, with investigators quickly amassing "overwhelming evidence" linking the 26-year-old to the murder. For many, the only lingering question was, "What might the defense of one of the most famous murder suspects in America look like?"

The answer, revealed in a single, dramatic federal court hearing on a recent Friday, proved to be as audacious as the crime itself: a full-throated embrace of guilt. Mangione, through his legal team, launched a high-stakes legal gambit, admitting to the killing in intricate detail. This surprising move, rather than signaling a swift end to the legal saga, appears to be a calculated maneuver designed to upend a looming state murder trial, embodying a risky defense strategy that his lawyers evidently believe is his best bet.

## The Crime, The Evidence, and Dual Prosecutions

[[SUM]] The assassination of United Healthcare CEO Brian Thompson led to formidable evidence against Luigi Mangione, including the murder weapon and a manifesto, resulting in both state and federal charges, with federal prosecutors eventually seeking the death penalty due to political tensions.

The assassination of Brian Thompson, a United Healthcare CEO, quickly became a national story. From the outset, the evidence against Mangione was formidable. At the scene, shell casings were recovered. Upon his arrest in Altoona, Pennsylvania, prosecutors claimed his backpack contained the murder weapon, a notebook detailing his plans, and a "manifesto" decrying the healthcare insurance industry. Viral videos of the shooting further cemented the public perception of his guilt.

Luigi Mangione soon found himself facing a complex legal battle on two fronts: state and federal. The Manhattan District Attorney's Office almost immediately charged him with 11 counts, including terrorism and second-degree murder. Shortly after, federal prosecutors from the U.S. Justice Department brought four counts, notably "use of a firearm to commit murder," which carried a potential death penalty, and two counts of interstate stalking resulting in Thompson's death, carrying a maximum sentence of life.

Initially, the federal government indicated it would defer to the state, allowing the Manhattan DA's office to try Mangione first. However, this changed after the election of President Trump, when his Attorney General, Pam Bondi, announced the administration would seek the death penalty. This decision not only accelerated the federal process but also highlighted a "natural competitiveness" between the two prosecutor's offices, exacerbated by political tensions. As reporter Benjamin Weiser of The New York Times explained, the U.S. Attorney's Office operated under the Trump administration's Justice Department, while the District Attorney, Alvin Bragg, had successfully prosecuted Donald Trump in 2024.

## The Defense's Initial Skirmishes

[[SUM]] Mangione's legal team successfully dropped state terrorism and federal death penalty charges, but their attempt to suppress evidence based on an alleged warrantless search had mixed results, with critical evidence like the murder weapon remaining admissible in the state case.

Facing such formidable odds and dual prosecutions, Mangione's legal team, led by seasoned New York lawyers Karen Friedman Agnifilo (a former number two prosecutor in the Manhattan DA's office) and Mark Agnifilo (known for representing high-profile defendants like Sean "Diddy" Combs), embarked on a multi-pronged defense.

Their initial strategy focused on attacking the charges themselves. Through vigorous arguments and filings in both state and federal courts, they achieved significant early victories. The state terrorism charge was successfully dropped, and crucially, the federal count carrying the potential death penalty was also dismissed.

Next, the defense turned its attention to suppressing the overwhelming evidence collected by investigators. Their argument centered on Mangione's arrest in Altoona, Pennsylvania. They contended that the initial search of his backpack was conducted without a warrant, violating his constitutional rights. Employing the "fruit of the poison tree" doctrine, they argued that any evidence derived from this allegedly illegal search should be inadmissible in court.

The outcome of these suppression motions, however, was mixed. The federal judge quickly ruled that under federal law, all the evidence was admissible. In the state case, the judge agreed to suppress some evidence but allowed two critical pieces to be admitted: the alleged murder weapon and the notebook detailing Mangione's plans. This meant that despite their efforts, much of the "overwhelming evidence" would still be presented against him at trial.

## The Shocking Confession: A Strategic Allocution

[[SUM]] With the state trial approaching, Mangione delivered a detailed allocution in federal court, pleading guilty to two stalking charges and explaining his premeditated actions against Thompson due to personal grievances with the healthcare system, a move seen as a strategic attempt to disrupt the state case.

With the state trial fast approaching – a judge had already announced an anonymous jury and set a September 8th date – Mangione's legal team appeared to be running out of options for a conventional defense. Then, an unexpected federal court notice appeared, scheduling a hearing for a Friday morning for undisclosed reasons. What transpired in that federal courtroom would send shockwaves through the legal community and the public.

Mangione, seated between his lawyers, delivered what is known as an "allocution"—a formal statement admitting to his actions. He pleaded guilty to the two federal stalking charges that led to Brian Thompson's death, and in doing so, offered a chillingly detailed account of his motives and the premeditated nature of the assassination.

He spoke of learning about United Healthcare's annual investor conference in New York City and emailing their leadership, posing as an investor managing over $50 billion in assets, to gain information. "Unlike my previous interactions with insurers," he quoted, "I received an immediate response within an hour." This, he suggested, highlighted the company's eagerness to respond to wealthy investors while, in his view, neglecting patients.

Mangione also revealed his personal grievances, mentioning "years of severe pain from what he said was a broken back" and his struggles navigating the health insurance system. These statements provided a direct glimpse into his motivation for targeting a healthcare executive. He further detailed using a 3D printer to make part of a gun, which he then equipped with a silencer and a magazine. He admitted traveling from outside New York to Manhattan "with the intent to shoot Brian Thompson."

His allocution culminated with a stark admission: "I shot Mr. Thompson in Manhattan and he died. When I did so, I understood that my actions would place him in fear of death and serious bodily injury. I knew what I was doing was illegal."

For reporter Haruka Mecca, who had followed the case for nearly two years, the immediate reaction was twofold: "It's remarkable to have all of this information after nearly 2 years to hear from him why he killed Brian Thompson and the details of the planning." Her "immediate second thought," however, was that "far from burning down the house that this was a strategic move from Mangione and his lawyers to upend the state case."

## The Double Jeopardy Gambit

[[SUM]] Immediately after his confession, Mangione's lawyers filed a motion to dismiss state murder charges based on double jeopardy, arguing that his federal plea for stalking resulting in death should preclude a second trial under New York's broader protections.

The strategic intent behind Mangione's detailed confession became clear within an hour of his plea. His lawyers promptly filed a motion in state court seeking to dismiss the state murder charges. Their primary argument: double jeopardy.

Double jeopardy is a constitutional protection preventing an individual from being tried twice for the same crime. However, as Weiser and Mecca explained, the application here is complex. The federal charges (interstate stalking resulting in death) and the state charges (second-degree murder) are technically different. The Manhattan District Attorney's office is expected to argue this distinction, asserting that the federal and state cases address "different harms."

But Mangione's defense is banking on New York state law, which offers broader protections for defendants than federal law. # 모두가 행복한 음악 페스티벌을 위한 에티켓: '그 사람'이 되지 않는 현명한 방법

### 에피소드 요약

음악 페스티벌에서 타인에게 불쾌감을 주지 않고 즐거운 시간을 보내기 위한 에티켓에 대해 NPR 팟캐스트의 아이샤 해리스, 스티븐 톰슨, 이사벨라 고메즈 사르미엔토가 논한다. 이들은 특히 앞줄 사수와 휴대폰 사용에 대한 잘못된 인식을 지적하며, 앞줄은 아티스트를 보기에 가장 좋은 자리가 아니라고 주장한다. 스티븐은 앞줄에서 좋은 자리를 차지하기 위해 소변을 참는 등의 극단적인 행동까지 서슴지 않는 최근의 사례를 언급하며, 앞줄은 바리케이드에 짓눌리고 주변 사람들의 불만을 사기 쉬우며, 음향 부스 뒤편처럼 오히려 더 좋은 시야와 공간을 확보할 수 있는 곳이 많다고 설명한다. 또한, 과도한 휴대폰 사용은 현장 경험을 방해하고 타인에게 불편을 줄 뿐만 아니라, 코첼라에서 마돈나가 깜짝 등장했을 때 관객들이 모두 휴대폰만 들고 촬영하는 모습처럼 아티스트에게도 좋지 않은 경험을 제공한다고 이사벨라는 강조한다.

이러한 논의를 통해 청취자들은 음악 페스티벌을 더욱 즐겁고 배려심 있게 경험하기 위한 실질적인 행동 지침을 얻을 수 있다. 앞줄에 대한 집착을 버리고 뒤쪽이나 개방된 공간에서 춤추고 친구들과 어울리는 것이 더 즐거운 경험이 될 수 있으며, 휴대폰은 비상시에만 사용하고 대부분의 시간 동안 주머니에 넣어두어 현장의 분위기와 소통에 집중하는 것이 좋다. 또한, 많은 인원이 함께 이동하며 타인의 시야나 공간을 방해하지 않도록, 세 명 이하의 소규모 그룹으로 이동하거나 친구들과 만날 때는 깃발이나 독특한 모자 등 아날로그적인 방법을 활용하여 서로를 찾는 것이 현명하다. 결국 페스티벌은 스트레스가 아닌 즐거움을 위한 공간이며, 타인을 배려하고 현재에 몰입하는 것이 가장 중요하다고 패널들은 입을 모아 말한다.

뜨거운 여름, 음악 애호가들의 마음을 설레게 하는 음악 페스티벌의 계절이 돌아왔습니다. 수많은 아티스트를 한자리에서 만날 수 있는 꿈같은 경험이지만, 때로는 일부 관객들의 지나친 행동으로 인해 즐거운 축제 분위기가 흐려지기도 합니다. NPR 팟캐스트 '팝 컬처 해피 아워(Pop Culture Happy Hour)'에서는 호스트 아이샤 해리스(Aisha Harris)와 공동 진행자 스티븐 톰슨(Stephen Thompson), NPR 음악 프로듀서 이사벨라 고메즈 사르미엔토(Isabella Gomez Sarmiento)가 모여 모두가 행복한 페스티벌을 위한 '에티켓'에 대해 심도 깊은 대화를 나눴습니다. 그들이 제시하는 현명한 축제 즐기기 방법을 통해 '민폐 관객'이 아닌 '모범 관객'으로 거듭나 보는 건 어떨까요?

## "앞줄 사수"는 환상일 뿐: 과도한 집착을 버려라

[[SUM]] 많은 이들이 무대 앞 최전방을 최고의 자리로 꼽지만, 전문가들은 이 자리가 과대평가되어 있다고 지적합니다.

음악 페스티벌에서 가장 좋은 자리는 어디일까요? 많은 이들이 무대 바로 앞, 이른바 '최전방'을 꼽겠지만, 전문가들은 이 자리가 생각보다 '과대평가'되어 있다고 입을 모읍니다.

### "실례를 무릅쓰고라도...?" 충격적인 앞줄 사수 사례

[[SUM]] 일부 관객들이 앞줄 사수를 위해 화장실 가는 것조차 포기하고 그 자리에서 용변을 해결하는 충격적인 사례가 발생하고 있으며, 이는 '최고의 명당'에 대한 극단적인 집착입니다.

스티븐 톰슨은 최근 해외 콘서트 현장에서 논란이 되고 있는 충격적인 사례를 언급하며 이야기를 시작합니다. 일부 관객들이 무대 앞 좋은 자리를 잃지 않기 위해 화장실 가는 것조차 포기하고 그 자리에서 용변을 해결하는 일이 벌어지고 있다는 것입니다. 올리비아 로드리고(Olivia Rodrigo)나 노아 카한(Noah Kahan) 같은 유명 아티스트들조차 이러한 현상에 대해 언급하며 우려를 표했습니다. 스티븐은 "의학적 문제가 아닌, 단순히 '최고의 명당'을 사수하기 위한 행동"이라며 이러한 극단적인 집착을 지적했습니다.

### 앞줄이 최악인 이유: 시야, 음향, 안전

[[SUM]] 앞줄은 바리케이드에 몸이 눌리고 시야 확보가 어려우며, 스피커 아래에서 청력 손상을 유발하고, 과도한 인파로 인해 탈수 등 안전 문제가 발생할 수 있습니다.

사실 앞줄은 여러 면에서 가장 좋은 자리가 아닐 수 있습니다.
*   **물리적 불편함:** 바리케이드에 몸이 눌리고, 주변 사람들의 불쾌한 시선과 숨결을 견뎌야 합니다. 아이샤는 키가 작은 사람의 경우, 아무리 앞줄에 있어도 수많은 인파에 가려 시야 확보가 어렵다고 설명합니다.
*   **음향 왜곡 및 청력 손상:** 스티븐은 너무 앞줄에 있으면 거대한 스피커 바로 아래에 위치하게 되어 최적의 사운드를 듣기 어렵다고 강조합니다. 그는 과거 다이노소어 주니어(Dinosaur Jr.) 공연에서 적절한 귀 보호 장치 없이 너무 가까이 있었던 탓에 30년이 지난 지금도 청력 손상에 시달리고 있다고 경고하며, 소중한 청력을 영구적으로 손상시킬 수 있다고 조언합니다.
*   **안전 문제:** 아이샤는 켄드릭 라마(Kendrick Lamar) 공연에서 앞줄에 있다가 친구가 탈수로 쓰러진 경험을 공유하며, 과도한 인파 속에서 안전 문제가 발생할 수 있음을 지적했습니다.

### 진정한 명당은 따로 있다

[[SUM]] 사운드보드 뒤편은 최적의 음향과 시야를 제공하며, 뒤쪽이나 개방된 공간은 친구들과 춤추고 스트레스 없이 편안하게 음악을 즐길 수 있는 진정한 명당입니다.

그렇다면 어디가 최고의 자리일까요?
*   **사운드보드 주변:** 스티븐은 사운드보드(soundboard) 뒤편이 의외의 명당이 될 수 있다고 말합니다. 시야가 방해받지 않으면서도 최적의 음향을 경험할 수 있는 경우가 많기 때문입니다.
*   **뒤쪽 또는 개방된 공간:** 이사벨라는 뒤쪽이나 좀 더 개방된 공간이 훨씬 즐겁다고 말합니다. 친구들과 함께 춤출 공간이 충분하고, 사람들에게 방해받지 않으면서 음악을 더 잘 감상할 수 있으며, 인파에 휩쓸릴 걱정이 없기 때문입니다. 스트레스 없이 편안하게 사람들과 어울릴 수 있는 것이야말로 페스티벌의 진정한 즐거움입니다.

## 스마트폰은 잠시 넣어두세요: '지금'을 경험하라

[[SUM]] 페스티벌은 세상과 단절하고 현재에 몰입할 기회이므로, 스마트폰 사용을 자제하고 '지금'을 경험하는 것이 중요합니다.

현대인의 필수품인 스마트폰. 하지만 페스티벌에서는 잠시 내려놓는 것이 어떨까요? 이사벨라와 스티븐은 페스티벌에서의 과도한 스마트폰 사용에 대해 강한 비판의 목소리를 냅니다.

### "콘텐츠 농사"를 멈추고 몰입의 즐거움을

[[SUM]] 공연 내내 스마트폰으로 촬영만 하는 '콘텐츠 양산'은 아티스트와의 단절과 라이브 음악의 공동체적 경험 상실을 초래합니다.

이사벨라는 페스티벌이 "세상과 단절하고 현재에 몰입할 수 있는 몇 안 되는 기회"라고 강조합니다. 하지만 많은 사람들이 공연 내내 스마트폰으로 촬영만 하고 있으며, 이는 "콘텐츠 양산(content farming)"에 가깝다고 지적합니다.
*   **아티스트와의 단절:** 올해 코첼라(Coachella)에서 사브리나 카펜터(Sabrina Carpenter)가 마돈나(Madonna)를 깜짝 게스트로 불렀을 때, 관객들이 모두 휴대폰을 들고 촬영만 하는 모습이 포착되어 이사벨라를 슬프게 했습니다. 그녀는 아티스트가 움직이지도, 노래하지도 않고 휴대폰만 들이대는 관객들을 보며 공연하는 것이 얼마나 힘들지 상상하기 어렵다고 말합니다.
*   **공동체 경험 상실:** 모두가 각자의 휴대폰 화면만 바라보는 것은 라이브 음악의 공동체적 경험을 방해합니다. 옆 사람과 소통하고 함께 춤추고 노래하는 즐거움을 놓치게 됩니다.

### 왜 굳이 스마트폰이 필요 없을까?

[[SUM]] 페스티벌은 통신 환경이 열악하고, 전문 비디오그래퍼들이 이미 고품질로 기록하며, NPR '타이니 데스크 콘서트'처럼 '지금 이 순간'에 집중하도록 휴대폰 사용을 통제하는 사례도 있습니다.

*   **열악한 통신 환경:** 대규모 인파가 몰리는 페스티벌에서는 통신 신호가 매우 약해져 휴대폰이 제대로 작동하지 않는 경우가 많습니다. 스티븐은 "포켓몬 고(Pokémon Go)"를 하려 해도 신호가 잡히지 않아 결국 휴대폰을 주머니에 넣어두게 된다는 유머러스한 경험담을 전합니다.
*   **전문가의 기록:** 이미 많은 공연들이 전문 비디오그래퍼들에 의해 고품질로 기록되고 있습니다. 아이샤는 "전문가들이 이미 자기 일을 하고 있는데, 왜 흔들리고 화질 나쁜 내 영상을 남겨야 하는가?"라고 반문합니다. 나중에 다시 찾아보는 경우도 거의 없다고 덧붙입니다.
*   **NPR의 '타이니 데스크 콘서트' 정책:** 이사벨라와 스티븐은 NPR의 '타이니 데스크 콘서트(Tiny Desk Concert)'에서 휴대폰 사용 정책을 예로 듭니다. 아티스트가 등장하면 모두가 사진 한 장을 찍게 한 후, 공연 내내 휴대폰을 주머니에 넣도록 보안 요원이 통제한다는 것입니다. 이는 관객들이 '지금 이 순간'에 집중하도록 하기 위함입니다.

물론 비상 상황이나 친구를 찾는 데 휴대폰이 필요할 수 있습니다. 짧은 시간 개인적인 추억을 담는 영상도 의미가 있습니다. 하지만 99%의 시간 동안은 휴대폰 없이도 충분히 페스티벌을 즐길 수 있습니다. 타인의 경험을 방해하지 않고 온전히 축제를 만끽하기 위해 잠시 디지털 세상에서 로그아웃하는 용기가 필요합니다.

## 떼 지어 이동하는 '기차놀이'는 그만: 모두를 위한 배려

[[SUM]] 여러 명이 줄지어 이동하는 '기차놀이'는 다른 관객들에게 길을 비켜주느라 공연을 놓치게 하고 교통 체증을 유발하여 큰 불편을 줍니다.

수많은 인파 속에서 길을 가로질러 이동하는 것도 중요한 에티켓입니다. 특히 여러 명이 함께 움직일 때 타인에게 큰 불편을 줄 수 있습니다.

### "길 좀 비켜주세요" 그 한마디가 불러오는 불편함

[[SUM]] 한 번에 최대 3명까지만 이동하는 것이 이상적이며, 그 이상은 타인의 즐거움을 방해하고 인파 속에서 불필요한 불안감을 유발할 수 있습니다.

아이샤는 "절대 양보할 수 없는 원칙(hill I would die on)"으로 '떼 지어 이동하지 않기'를 꼽습니다. 한두 명이 지나가는 것은 괜찮지만, 10명 이상이 줄지어 지나가면 마치 '기차놀이'처럼 보여 다른 관객들에게 극심한 불편을 초래한다는 것입니다. 사람들은 길을 비켜주느라 공연을 놓치거나, 부딪히고, 복잡한 교통 체증을 유발하게 됩니다.
*   **최대 3명:** 아이샤는 한 번에 최대 3명까지만 이동하는 것이 이상적이라고 조언합니다. 3명 정도는 15~20초 내에 지나갈 수 있어 큰 불편을 주지 않기 때문입니다. 그 이상이 되면 타인의 즐거움을 방해하는 행위가 됩니다.
*   **불필요한 불안감:** 스티븐은 인파 속에서 너무 빽빽하게 갇히는 것을 싫어해 항상 자신 앞에 약간의 공간을 두는 편이라고 말합니다. 이는 일종의 '탈출 전략'이자 '불안감 해소'를 위한 것인데, 긴 줄의 사람들이 그 공간을 계속 침범하면 결국 그가 공간을 만들었던 이유, 즉 불안감이 다시 커지게 된다고 토로합니다.

### 똑똑하게 친구 찾는 법: 아날로그의 지혜

[[SUM]] 긴 막대 끝에 인형을 달거나 독특한 모자를 쓰는 등 아날로그적인 방법으로 친구를 찾고, 그룹이 흩어지는 것을 자연스럽게 받아들이는 것이 현명합니다.

그렇다면 대규모 그룹과 함께 온 경우 친구들을 어떻게 찾아야 할까요? 아이샤는 아날로그적인 방법을 제안합니다.
*   **긴 막대 끝에 인형:** 한 명이 긴 막대 끝에 커다란 인형을 달아 들고 다니면 멀리서도 쉽게 알아볼 수 있습니다.
*   **독특한 모자:** 키가 큰 친구가 눈에 띄는 독특한 모자를 쓰는 것도 좋은 방법입니다.

이사벨라는 "페스티벌에서는 그룹이 흩어지는 것이 자연스러운 일"이라며, "친구를 찾거나 인파를 헤치고 나아가느라 공연 내내 스트레스받는 것만큼 힘든 일은 없다"고 말합니다. 잠시 멈춰 서서 음악을 즐기고, 공연이 끝난 후 다시 만나는 것이 현명한 방법입니다. 아이샤는 3명 이상이 지나가려 하면 "더 이상은 안 된다"며 길을 막아서는 것도 불사한다고 말하며, 그만큼 이 문제가 중요하다고 강조했습니다.

## 결론: "그냥 존재하라(Just Exist)" - 축제의 본질을 찾아서

[[SUM]] 페스티벌 에티켓의 본질은 '친절함'과 '배려'로, 휴대폰이나 앞줄에 집착하지 않고 그 순간에 몰입하여 음악과 공동체의 즐거움을 만끽하는 것입니다.

세 명의 패널이 공통적으로 내리는 결론은 "그냥 존재하라(Just Exist)"는 것입니다. 휴대폰에 갇히거나, 앞줄에 집착하거나, 인파를 헤치고 다니는 대신, 그저 그 순간에 몰입하여 축제의 본질을 즐기는 것입니다.

*   **친절함과 배려:** 스티븐은 페스티벌이 "즐겁고 좋은 곳이어야 한다"며, "열사병에 걸리고, 탈수되고, 청력을 손상시키는 비참한 캠프가 되어서는 안 된다"고 강조합니다.
*   **소통과 교류:** 이사벨라는 "주변 사람들과 눈을 마주치고 대화하며 친구를 만들라"고 조언합니다. 낯선 이들과의 교류 또한 페스티벌 경험의 중요한 부분이기 때문입니다.

결국 음악 페스티벌 에티켓은 '친절함'과 '배려'로 귀결됩니다. 모두가 서로를 존중하고, 현재의 순간에 집중하며, 음악과 공동체의 즐거움을 만끽한다면, 음악 페스티벌은 모두에게 잊지 못할 행복한 추억을 선사할 것입니다. 이제 '그 사람'이 아닌 '우리 모두'가 즐거운 축제를 만들어갈 때입니다.
