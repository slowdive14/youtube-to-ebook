# Min Jin Lee's Decade-Long Journey: Unpacking the Costs of the American Dream in 'American Hagwon'

### Episode summary

Min Jin Lee, author of the acclaimed novel *Pachinko*, discusses her newest work, *American Hagwon*, which explores an upwardly mobile Korean family's economic destabilization and their pursuit of the American dream through education. Lee explains that a "hagwon" is a Korean learning center, often a cram school, but also a place for various skills, becoming a crucial, yet financially burdensome, part of Korean culture, leading to issues like a negative birth rate due to high costs.

Lee reveals that writing *American Hagwon* was a transformative decade-long process, allowing her to "make cosmos out of chaos" and reassemble her thoughts. She cautions against the intense pressure on children to attend "name-brand" schools, highlighting the heartbreak of parents spending hard-earned money on a system that doesn't guarantee success or happiness, and often overlooks the child's own desires.

Min Jin Lee has solidified her place as an author whose new releases are eagerly anticipated events. Her 2017 epic, *Pachinko*, captivated the literary world, earning rave reviews, a National Book Award nomination, and a television adaptation. Now, Lee returns with her highly anticipated follow-up, *American Hagwon*, a novel that delves into the profound questions of success, sacrifice, and belonging in a rapidly changing world. In a recent interview with NPR's Scott Simon, Lee offered deep insights into the themes that shaped her latest work and the transformative journey of writing it.

*American Hagwon* introduces the Koh family – John, Helen, and their three children, Bo, D.H., and Mina – an upwardly mobile South Korean family whose comfortable middle-class existence is upended by the 1997 Asian financial crisis. Forced to leave Seoul for Southern California, they confront a new culture brimming with possibilities, but also the daunting challenge of navigating the American dream from an economically destabilized position. The novel, Lee explains, grapples with fundamental questions: "What does it take to make it, and is it really worth it?"

### Beyond the Cram School: Defining the 'Hagwon'

[[SUM]] A "hagwon" is a Korean learning center for anything outside traditional schooling, from cooking to test prep; Min Jin Lee herself attended an American "hagwon" in the form of Princeton Review.

[[AT:1j6mhNFMPRM:159]]

At the heart of the novel is the concept of the "hagwon," a term Lee elaborates on for the American audience. "A Hagwon is the Korean word for learning center," she explains. While often associated with intense cram schools for entrance exams, the term encompasses a much broader spectrum of learning, from gourmet cooking classes and tap dancing to piano lessons. It is, she emphasizes, "a very important part of Korean culture," a place where one goes to learn anything outside of traditional schooling.

Lee herself, surprisingly, never attended a formal Korean hagwon. Yet, she found her own "hagwon of America" in the form of test prep. Recalling her high school days at the Bronx High School of Science, she recounted, "I did want to go to a college of my dreams... And I knew that I had to have a good score." Her parents, despite the financial strain, found the $300-$500 needed for her to attend Princeton Review. She draws a clear parallel: American institutions like Princeton Review, Stanley Kaplan, or Kumon, catering to everything from law school to MCATs, are essentially American hagwons. This personal connection underscores the universal nature of intense, supplementary education, transcending cultural boundaries.

### The Shattered Dream: A Family's Fall from Grace

[[SUM]] The Koh family's middle-class security shatters after the 1997 Asian financial crisis, forcing them to move to Southern California and confront the harsh reality of becoming working-class.

[[AT:1j6mhNFMPRM:190]]

The Koh family's experience in Southern California vividly illustrates the emotional and psychological toll of economic upheaval. John and Helen, once white-collar professionals, find themselves in a small apartment with "linoleum floors that I think they say are possibly once yellow." Lee describes the experience of "coming down in the world" as common, but profoundly shocking for the children who had long been protected by their solidly middle-class status. Seeing their parents become working-class people shatters their sense of security and confidence. Mina's hopeful declaration, "We won't be here long," reflects a youthful optimism that quickly confronts harsh realities.

### The Pressure Cooker of Performance: A Global Phenomenon

[[SUM]] The intense competitiveness in education, driven by the belief that "name brand schools" are the only path to success, is a global issue that often breaks children and families, contributing to Korea's negative birth rate.

[[AT:1j6mhNFMPRM:314]]

As Mina begins working at a hagwon in Southern California, she observes a disturbing trend among her students. "These kids seem a bit distorted about what all of this means," Lee notes. This observation leads to a broader commentary on the escalating competitiveness in education. "The environment has become so competitive," Lee states, "and the children who want to go to the highly competitive schools have to become so professional very early on."

This isn't merely a Korean issue, Lee argues, but an "American issue for middle-class families" driven by the belief that a "name brand school" is the sole path to success and safety. Yet, Lee challenges this notion with a sobering reality: "It's not true. Like, you could go to a fancy school and not have things work out." She points out that most people can readily recall numerous examples of individuals who attended prestigious institutions but did not achieve the life they, or others, expected. Moreover, pushing a child into a school they don't want to attend is rarely beneficial.

Lee acknowledges the popular criticism of Korean education as "stupid and it's evil and it's wrong," and supports the right to hold such beliefs. However, she offers a nuanced perspective: "the general competence and the abilities of Korean children are really high." This intense educational "push," she concedes, contributed to the "incredible development of a very, very poor country in a tiny amount of time." The darker side, however, is undeniable: "many other children and families are broken by this kind of stress." A striking consequence, she highlights, is Korea's negative birth rate, with "hagwon bills" often cited as a primary reason people cannot afford to have children.

### The Burden of Global Ambition and Willful Self-Sacrifice

[[SUM]] Korean families pursue "global success" to an "Olympic medalist level," a relentless and often unfair competition intertwined with the cultural demand for "willful self-sacrifice," exemplified by the tale of Shim Chung.

[[AT:1j6mhNFMPRM:500]]

The novel's scope extends globally, following Korean families across South Korea, Australia, Brazil, and the US, revealing a pervasive drive for "global success." Lee was particularly struck by the ambition of young Koreans in Korea who aspire to be "globally competitive," almost to an "Olympic medalist level of everything." She finds this pursuit challenging, recognizing that the competition is often unfair and success elusive for the vast majority. "I don't know if that can yield a great deal of enjoyment," Lee muses. "When do you enjoy it? Because even if you get there, then don't you have to go to the next thing?"

This relentless pursuit is deeply intertwined with the theme of "willful self-sacrifice," a concept Scott Simon astutely identifies in the novel. Lee agrees, stating it's "something that's really demanded in the culture of Korea." She illustrates this with the poignant fairy tale of Shim Chung, the blind man's daughter who sells herself to sailors to secure rice for a monastery, hoping the monks' prayers will restore her father's sight. This story, rooted in filial piety and Confucianism, is held up as the ideal virtue of a good son or daughter. While open to criticism, Lee emphasizes the philosophical pillars that make such sacrifice profoundly important to Korean culture.

### A Decade of Transformation: Making Cosmos Out of Chaos

[[SUM]] Min Jin Lee spent a decade writing *American Hagwon*, a transformative process that allowed her to make "cosmos out of chaos" by meticulously ordering disparate elements to build her novel's world.

[[AT:1j6mhNFMPRM:624]]

*American Hagwon* was a monumental undertaking, taking Min Jin Lee a full decade to write. "I actually feel so grateful that it's done," she shares. The extensive process, however, was deeply transformative. "The act of writing a book really will transform you," Lee asserts. "I'm a different person than I was before I wrote the book, and it's not just the years passing, it's actually what I learned and also the ability to make cosmos out of chaos." She describes starting with questions, then meticulously reassembling and ordering "all the disparate elements in my mind" to arrive at the answers and build the world of her novel.

Through the Koh family's struggles and triumphs, Min Jin Lee invites readers to explore the intricate tapestry of ambition, cultural expectations, and personal sacrifice. *American Hagwon* promises to be a powerful and timely examination of what it truly means to make it, and at what cost, in an increasingly competitive global landscape.

# Red State Revelations, Blue State Blues: The Unforeseen Twists in the Battle for the Senate

### Episode summary

The New York Times' "Midterm Surprise" reveals Democrats are performing "remarkably well" in deep-red states, challenging conventional wisdom for the upcoming midterm elections. Polling in five states Trump won by 11+ points (Ohio, Texas, Iowa, Alaska, Kansas) shows Democrats leading in three and tied in one, a phenomenon attributed to President Trump's "shockingly bad" 40% approval rating in these states and strong Democratic candidates like Kansas's Adam Hamilton, a popular pastor.

This unexpected surge in red states is contrasted by Democrats struggling in traditionally blue states like Maine and Michigan, where strong Republican candidates like Susan Collins or complicated Democratic candidates like Abdul El-Sayed are creating tighter races. The takeaway is that in a politically disillusioned era, voters are prioritizing "candidate quality" and personal integrity over party affiliation, gravitating towards individuals they trust, regardless of their party's typical leanings.

As the midterm elections loom, the battle for control of the U.S. Senate presents a political landscape far more complex and counterintuitive than expected. New polling data from the New York Times reveals a surprising trend: Democratic candidates are performing remarkably well in deeply conservative "red states" that were once considered out of reach, while simultaneously facing unexpected challenges in traditionally "blue" or swing states that should, in theory, be easier wins. This paradoxical situation, explored by chief political analyst Nate Cohn, suggests a fundamental shift in voter behavior, driven largely by candidate quality and a pervasive dissatisfaction with both major parties.

## The Red State Revelation: Democrats' Uphill Climb Becomes a Sprint

[[SUM]] Democrats are surprisingly competitive in five deeply conservative "red states"—Ohio, Texas, Iowa, Alaska, and Kansas—with leads in three, a tied race in one, and a one-point deficit in another, a historic shift from traditional expectations.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:62]]

For Democrats to flip control of the U.S. Senate, the conventional wisdom held that they would need to secure victories in states that, in Cohn's words, "Democrats have no business winning." This meant triumphing in at least two states that Donald Trump carried by 11 points or more in the 2024 presidential election – states that traditionally lean heavily Republican in federal contests. Such an endeavor has historically been an uphill battle, given the entrenched partisan divides of recent decades.

However, recent polling from five such red states – Ohio, Texas, Iowa, Alaska, and Kansas – paints an extraordinary picture. All five voted for Trump by at least 11 points in 2024, yet Democrats are proving highly competitive across the board. The polls show Democrats with meaningful leads in Texas, Alaska, and Ohio, a tied race in Kansas, and Republicans holding only a nominal one-point edge in Iowa.

This level of competitiveness is truly historic. It has been 16 years since the Democratic Party managed to win even one state leaning so heavily toward the opposition in a regularly scheduled Senate election. To now contemplate the possibility of winning not just two, but potentially more, is unprecedented in this era of polarized politics. This phenomenon strongly suggests that Republican unpopularity, particularly at the presidential level, is effectively transforming deep red states into unexpected battlegrounds.

### The Trump Factor: A Drag on the Ticket

[[SUM]] A key reason for Democratic strength in these red states is former President Trump's strikingly low 40% approval rating, a significant drop from his 2024 performance, indicating widespread dissatisfaction among his traditional base.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:251]]

A primary driver of Democratic strength in these conservative bastions is the profound unpopularity of the former president. While negative numbers for Trump are no longer surprising, his approval rating across these five states stands at a strikingly low 40%. This is particularly significant given that he won these same states with an average of 56% of the vote in the last election.

This stark drop indicates that a substantial segment of the traditional Republican coalition in these states is deeply dissatisfied with Trump's performance. Voters express discontent across a range of issues, including the economy, foreign policy (specifically, the war), affordability, gas prices, and immigration. Compounding this, Trump's approval rating in these states has dropped five points since June, indicating a dangerous downward trend as the campaign season intensifies. Such a significant decline for an incumbent president in reliably solid states hasn't been seen since George W. Bush in 2006 or 2008.

### Candidate Quality: The Democratic Edge

[[SUM]] Democrats benefit from strong candidates who are viewed more favorably than their Republican opponents, often perceived as having better moral character and being less ideologically extreme, even in conservative states.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:342]]

Beyond Trump's unpopularity, the Democrats' success in these red states is heavily attributed to their ability to recruit and nominate strong candidates, often against weaker Republican opponents. In all five of these competitive red states, more voters hold a favorable view of the Democratic candidate than an unfavorable one. Conversely, for their Republican counterparts, the unfavorable views outweigh the favorable ones – a remarkable finding for traditionally red states where Republicans typically outnumber Democrats.

This "candidate quality advantage" extends across multiple dimensions. Democrats lead in all five states on the question of who possesses "better moral character," a judgment reflecting personal integrity. Even more surprisingly, despite voters in these states generally perceiving the Democratic Party as "too far to the left," they are more likely to view the Republican candidate as "too far to the right." This suggests that Democrats have successfully nominated individuals who are both personally compelling and ideologically acceptable, despite the conservative leanings of their states.

Consider **Adam Hamilton** in Kansas, the most Republican state in the surveyed group. Hamilton, a pastor of the largest United Methodist congregation in the country, exemplifies this strategy. His profession naturally imbues him with an image of good moral character, and he is less likely to be perceived as an ideological radical. Crucially, he lacks an electoral track record that Republicans could exploit, making him "a very challenging person for them to run against." His Republican opponent, incumbent **Roger Marshall**, has underperformed in the past and faces significant liabilities, including reports of pursuing medical debts against low-income Kansans to the point of arrest, a point now highlighted in Democratic advertisements.

In Texas, the dynamic is even more pronounced. While Hamilton is the most popular Democratic candidate, Republican **Ken Paxton** is the least popular candidate across all the polls. Paxton's political woes are extensive and well-known, including impeachment by his own party, federal investigations, and a messy divorce. Even former President Trump, who endorsed Paxton, has publicly ridiculed him. The Democratic candidate, James Telerico, holds a commanding 23-point advantage over Paxton on "moral character," underscoring how Paxton's "profoundly flawed" nature has put his party in grave danger in what has long been one of the nation's most reliably Republican states.

This erosion of Republican support is multifaceted. One "layer" involves younger and Latino voters who may have supported Trump in 2024 but are now disillusioned. They voted for promises of "no wars, low prices," only to see rising costs and foreign entanglements. This has led to a significant reversion to Democratic support, particularly impactful in Texas, a state with a large Latino and young population. James Telerico, for instance, garners over 60% of the vote among Latino voters, leading by nearly 30 points in that demographic alone, effectively making the state a battleground.

A second, deeper "layer" involves traditional Republican voters. It's crucial to remember that all five of these red states voted *against* Donald Trump in the 2016 Republican primary. These are states with a higher proportion of voters who align with the traditional Republicanism of George W. Bush, Mitt Romney, and Ronald Reagan. Many of these voters, though generally voting Republican, were never entirely comfortable with Trump's conduct or policies. Now, with the political climate deteriorating, their reservations are manifesting as a willingness to consider Democratic candidates, causing the "floor to fall out" beneath Republican candidates in these states.

## Blue State Blues: Unexpected Headwinds for Democrats

[[SUM]] Paradoxically, Democrats face unexpected challenges in traditionally blue or purple states like Maine, Michigan, and New Hampshire, due to strong moderate Republican candidates or problematic Democratic nominees.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:838]]

Paradoxically, while Democrats are making historic inroads in red states, they are simultaneously encountering unexpected difficulties in several traditionally "blue" or purple states that they might have taken for granted. Polling in Maine, Michigan, and New Hampshire reveals that despite the overall favorable political environment for Democrats, these races remain surprisingly competitive.

The explanation for this counterintuitive trend again boils down to candidate quality, but in an inverted fashion. In these bluer states, Republicans have either nominated strong, relatively moderate candidates with established local support, or Democrats have put forward candidates who risk alienating portions of their own base.

### Maine's Maverick: Susan Collins' Enduring Appeal

[[SUM]] In Maine, Republican Senator Susan Collins leads by three points despite the state's Democratic lean, largely because voters like her and view her as effective, while disliking the Democratic challenger, Troy Jackson.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:933]]

Maine, the most Democratic of the states polled (Kamala Harris won it by seven points in 2024), presents the most striking example of this "blue state blues." Republican Senator **Susan Collins** actually led the poll by three points. The story is simple: while Maine voters overwhelmingly disapprove of President Trump and desire Democratic control of the Senate, they genuinely like Susan Collins and dislike the Democratic challenger, Troy Jackson.

Collins scores well on nearly every metric, seen as an effective senator who isn't "too far to the right" despite representing a blue state. Her support is built on decades of public service in Maine. The Democratic Party's hope was that the current political environment would finally be enough to unseat her, but these polls suggest that, for now, Maine voters are inclined to give Collins another term.

The Democratic candidate, **Troy Jackson**, faces significant headwinds. His rating in the poll showed 49% unfavorable to 41% favorable in a blue state, a stark contrast to other Democratic candidates. While his lack of charisma and an unusual convention-based nomination process might play a role, the precise reasons for his unpopularity remain "the single biggest mystery across these eight polls."

### Michigan's Muddled Message: Abdul El-Sayed's Strengths and Weaknesses

[[SUM]] Michigan's Democratic candidate, Abdul El-Sayed, holds a five-point lead but shows vulnerabilities, with strong youth support contrasted by underperformance among Black and older voters, costing Democrats half their generic ballot lead.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:1057]]

Michigan offers another case where the Democratic candidate's complexities pose a risk. Here, the issue isn't a strong Republican opponent, but rather a Democratic candidate with a mix of pronounced strengths and weaknesses. Democrat **Abdul El-Sayed** leads Republican Mike Rogers by five points (49-44) in the poll, but a deeper look reveals underlying vulnerabilities.

El-Sayed, a progressive candidate from the activist wing of the party, has strong support among young voters, showing an astounding 82-11 lead among 18-29 year olds – potentially "the biggest lead among young voters of any Siena poll ever conducted." This "electrifying" youth support is a significant asset.

However, El-Sayed also exhibits weaknesses among crucial Democratic voting blocs. He underperforms among Black voters, leading by 76-13, a respectable number but significantly lower than the 92-1 lead held by the Democratic gubernatorial candidate among the same demographic. This indicates a segment of Black voters willing to split their ticket, supporting Republicans for Senate while voting Democratic for other offices. Similarly, El-Sayed lags among older voters (over 45), a group where other Democrats are typically tied or ahead.

These mixed results mean that while El-Sayed holds a modest lead, his reliance on a potentially "fickle and difficult-to-reach voting block" (young voters) and his underperformance among other key demographics represent a risk. Critically, Democrats lead by 10 points on the generic ballot in Michigan, meaning El-Sayed's five-point lead represents an "underperformance," effectively costing Democrats half of the lead they should theoretically have in this race.

## The Shifting Senate Landscape: Paths to Control

[[SUM]] Democrats' path to Senate control depends on winning at least two deep red states; current polling suggests they could flip Ohio, Alaska, and Texas, potentially securing the Senate even if they lose Maine.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:1274]]

Given these complex and often contradictory findings, predicting the future control of the Senate is indeed "foolhardy." However, the polling data does illuminate potential paths for Democrats. The initial premise was that Democrats needed to win at least two states Trump carried by 11+ points. But if blue states like Maine (where Susan Collins currently leads) unexpectedly go Republican, Democrats would then need to secure *three* of those deep red states.

Based on the current polling, Democrats hold comfortable leads in Ohio, Alaska, and Texas – precisely three such states. If these polls hold true, Democrats could flip the Senate even if they were to lose Maine. Should they lose other states, victories in closer red states like Kansas or Iowa would become crucial. Conversely, if Democrats manage to win Maine, they could afford to lose one of the red states where they currently have a lead.

The overarching takeaway, regardless of the final outcome, is the "counterintuitive" reality that Democrats are making more significant gains in red states than in blue or swing states. In an astonishing twist, Democrats are polling better in Texas than in Michigan or Maine.

## Beyond Partisan Lines: A Search for Trust and Competence

[[SUM]] This election cycle highlights voters' widespread dissatisfaction with both parties, leading them to prioritize "candidate quality" and gravitate towards individuals perceived as trustworthy and not too closely tied to either party brand.

[[AT:6dBxDAYVEag:1398]]

This peculiar election cycle, if these polls are any indication, will be defined by "candidate quality." In an era where both major parties face widespread disillusionment – where the Biden presidency failed to deliver the promised "return to normalcy," and the Trump presidency failed to bring "low prices and an end to foreign wars" – voters are exhibiting a profound dissatisfaction with the status quo.

The findings suggest that voters are "rejecting both parties to a certain degree," gravitating instead "toward the candidate that they thought they could trust." This could mean supporting a moderate Republican with decades of experience in Maine or a Democratic pastor with a clean slate in Kansas. In states most likely to determine Senate control, the most successful candidates appear to be those "not too closely tied to, tethered to, associated with either" major party brand.

This sentiment reflects a deeper trend. It has been over 20 years since American voters expressed satisfaction with the direction of the country. They have tried both parties, and the persistent frustration is now manifesting in a weakening of familiar partisan patterns. As voters grapple with a sense of failure from their usual options, they are increasingly willing to "shake things up," seeking out individuals they believe can offer a different path, regardless of their party label or the state's traditional leanings. The midterm surprise, therefore, may not just be about who wins, but about *how* voters are choosing to cast their ballots in an increasingly unpredictable political landscape.

# 민진 이 작가, '아메리칸 학원'으로 10년의 여정을 마치다: 글쓰기가 선사한 변모

### 에피소드 요약

### 에피소드 요약
